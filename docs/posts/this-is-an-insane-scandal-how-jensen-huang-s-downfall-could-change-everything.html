‘This Is an Insane Scandal’: How Jensen Huang’s Downfall Could Change Everything

Jensen Huang, the founder and chief executive of Nvidia, resigned on Thursday after admitting to an affair with a Singapore AI executive who is suspected by US officials of being a spy for the Chinese government.

The downfall of arguably the single most important person in AI is a massive story. But the implications and insinuations of this scandal go deeper, touching on everything from American national security, US-China relations, the future of the AI buildout, chip export policies, your retirement account, and an existential debate among AI experts about who should have access to the most advanced AI chips.

CHAPTER 1: THE MAIN CHARACTERS
The CEO, the Spy, and the Singapore Question

Can you tell me the gist in one sentence?

The chief executive of Nvidia had multiple affairs1, including one with a suspected Chinese spy, who was an executive at a Singapore company that bought billions of dollars of Nvidia chips and is suspected of illegally redirecting them to Chinese companies in violation of American law.

Can you explain the larger stakes of the scandal in one sentence?

It’s probably something like this: It’s sketchy for chief executives to have affairs with their employees and major clients; sketchier still for them to have affairs with clients whose business is suspected of violating federal law; even sketchier for that paramour to also be a suspected secret agent of a foreign government; maximally sketchy if that foreign government is America’s top geopolitical adversary; and beyond all reckoning of sketchiness if the US government knew of these allegations and revelations and did approximately f*ck-all about it for months.

We’ll get to the nitty-gritty of everything in that sentence further down in the piece, but that’s basically what we’re dealing with here.

Backing up, just how irreplaceable is Jensen Huang?

It’s hard to imagine a single individual more central to the artificial intelligence age. Huang is the founder and chief executive of the world’s largest AI firm, whose sales account for roughly 80 percent of global AI chips

But even that pat description falls short of capturing what Huang means to the AI world. With his trademark black leather jacket and cult devotion, he is “the Taylor Swift of AI,” according to the Economist. In Taiwan, adoration of him is known as Jensanity. This rockstar status is financially useful. Analysts say Nvidia benefits from personal relationships with Taiwanese manufacturers who are eager to work with him, “hoping his stardust will rub off on them.” If Jensen is irreplaceable, it’s by design. He claims roughly 60 direct reports—up to 10-times more than the industry average—and has said he doesn’t believe in succession planning.

Huang is also unusually close to the Trump administration. He has met with President Donald Trump repeatedly in the last two years, joined the president on international trips, lobbied the administration on China chip policy, and even taken calls from the president during live events that showcased his closeness with the White House.

Since the first news reports about his affair with Alice Huang, Nvidia’s valuation has declined by more than $1 trillion.

Who is Alice Huang, and why does the U.S. think she’s a spy?

Alice Huang (no relation to Jensen, obviously) is a Chinese citizen. She spent years as a TV journalist before moving into finance. In 2023, she became a director at Megaspeed, a Singapore data center company that became one of the largest buyers of Nvidia chips in Southeast Asia.

Remarkably, the Wall Street Journal reported this week that her husband, a Chinese businessman, introduced her to Jensen around early 2024 and may have known about and tolerated the affair for some time.

There is no definitive proof that Huang is a spy. But after surveilling her communications, US agencies grew to suspect that she served as an asset of China’s Ministry of State Security. According to the New York Times, US officials believed she was instructed to glean sensitive information about Nvidia and US policy and to influence Huang’s views on selling AI chips to Chinese companies.

What is Megaspeed?

Megaspeed is a data center firm based in Singapore. It began as the Singapore arm of a Chinese gaming company and was renamed Megaspeed in 2023, after which it rapidly transformed into one of the biggest Nvidia customers in the world. According to several reports, its assets grew from about $33 million at the end of 2023 (when Alice Huang joined the company) to nearly $3 billion a year later. The firm has bought roughly $5 billion worth of Nvidia chips over roughly two years, making it one of the largest customers in Asia.

What’s the relationship between Megaspeed and China, and is it illegal?

This is where the plot really thickens.

Since 2022, the U.S. has blocked Nvidia from selling its best chips to China for the purpose of keeping China stuck in second place in the AI race. But the U.S. has permitted the sale of advanced AI chips to other Asian countries, such as Singapore and Malaysia. In the last few years, Nvidia sales to Southeast Asia have exploded.

Many AI experts and government officials are deeply uncomfortable with this. Megaspeed has maintained close ties to several Chinese companies, and critics have long suspected that Chinese firms are illegally using companies domiciled in places like Singapore to access restricted hardware—a practice known as “Singapore washing.”2 The Commerce Department spent months investigating Megaspeed for suspected smuggling of Nvidia chips into China. But the investigation was transferred to the Justice Department, after a curious email from a Nvidia lawyer.

What did the White House know, and when did it know it?

Now things start to get weird. Well, weirder.

The New York Times reports that a Commerce Department investigation into Megaspeed for suspected smuggling “came to a sudden halt” last year, after Nvidia’s general counsel directly contacted the investigators by email. Sources viewed the interaction as “violating government procedure and potentially tainting the case,” according to the Times.

While Commerce was investigating Megaspeed, US intelligence officials began to suspect Alice Huang of being a spy. Huang’s affair was “known by top officials, including Marco Rubio and CIA Director John Ratcliffe, going back to last year,” according to the Wall Street Journal. In the spring, Rubio oversaw the drafting of a classified memo warning about Jensen’s personal relationship.

In other words, when Jensen Huang joined the president on his trip to China in May and attended the White House state dinner with Chinese leader Xi Jinping in September, senior White House officials already suspected that Huang’s lover was a Chinese spy, while the administration was separately scrutinizing the relationship between her company and China.
