What are Tactical Decision Games?

In order to explain TDGs to you, I'm going to have to tell you a story.

After the Vietnam War, the four branches of the US military that were involved — the Army, the Marine Corps, the Navy, and the Air Force — did a period of soul-searching in response to their (fairly horrible) experiences. The Army and the Marine Corps, in particular, went through the most reflection because these two branches experienced the worst of the war.

This led to a number of reforms. The Army's reforms led to the Air-Land Battle doctrine, which was very successful and led to decisive victories in the 1990 Gulf War. The Marine Corps, on the other hand, adopted manoeuvre warfare as its doctrine. Colonel John Schmitt, under the direction of General Al Gray, wrote Marine Corps Doctrinal Publication Number 1 (MCDP 1): Warfighting, which remains the foundational doctrine document for Marine manoeuvre warfare to this day. If you're interested in what a decentralised organisation actually looks like, I recommend reading Warfighting; it's well written and not very long.

What is manoeuvre warfare? Manoeuvre warfare is a method of combat that allows a small fighting force to defeat a much larger one.

It does so by:

  • Setting tempo — forcing the enemy to respond to your execution speed, at a rate that leaves them disoriented
  • Focal point — attacking the enemy's critical point, at the right place and the right time
  • Surprise — which involves deception and sowing confusion, so as to attack the focal point at a decisive time, when the enemy least expects it
  • Combined arms — a combination of different unit types where counter-attack against any one type leaves the enemy vulnerable to attack from another type.
  • Flexibility — the fighting force must be able to adapt to conditions on the fly, improvising to create opportunity or take advantage of opportunities that present themselves in the chaos of the battlefield. It must also be designed to be well-rounded, self-contained and redundant, so that it may maintain different avenues of attack — either in movement, method, or some other factor. If multiple avenues of attack are constantly left open, alternatives are always available, which means opportunities can always be seized.
  • Decentralised command — each unit's orders will be accompanied by Commander's Intent — that is, what is the intention of the order? During battle, conditions may rapidly outpace the ability for central command to issue updated instructions. Therefore, units should be able to adapt to conditions to achieve the 'commander's intent' — the outcomes assigned to them without following the instructions of the order.

If all of this sounds like the ideas of John Boyd, that's because it is — Boyd was one of the influences.

Manoeuvre warfare requires improvisation and autonomy from lower-level units, and this creates a training challenge. How do you train soldiers to improvise on the fly in this decentralised manner? How do you help them make better decisions under the heat of battle?

The answer that Schmitt came up with was TDGs.

At its core, TDGs teach sensemaking in order to accelerate expertise. Put differently, it teaches novices to see as more experienced soldiers, so that they are able to spot tactical opportunities that they may exploit.

PS: As a side note, I believe Schmitt is the reason the science fiction novel Ender's Game is on the Marine Corps Commandant's Professional Reading List. First, because the fighting in Ender is a perfect demonstration of manoeuvre warfare. Second, because the key phrase "the enemy's gate is down" is a perfect demonstration of how important sensemaking is.

(Skip the next bit if you don't want spoilers for the book).

If you're not familiar with the book, Ender is a young boy who is recruited to train, with other children, in a 'battle school' — a space station orbiting around Earth. Their goal is to become good enough to bring the fight to aliens that very nearly destroyed Earth years ago. The children train in zero gravity battle rooms, where they fire stun guns at each other. The objective of the battle simulations is to have at least one member of your team enter through the opposing team's gate. Initially, everyone enters the zero-G room with their sense of 'down' carried over from the corridor outside. Ender's insight is that if you reframe the situation as "the enemy's gate is down", you can enter the battle room with your legs first, presenting a smaller target, plus you can use your legs as a shield, and you can come at the opposing army from angles they would never expect, giving you an advantage.
