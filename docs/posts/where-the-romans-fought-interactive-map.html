Where the Romans Fought (interactive map)

Proelia Romanorum · Μάχαι ῬωμαίωνBattles of the Romans · 753 BCE – 1475 CE

Every named land and sea battle fought by the Romans, from the wars of Romulus to the fall of Theodoro in 1475: the Kingdom, the Republic, the Empire, West and East as one state, and after 1204 its successor states. Where each was fought, against whom, who won, and who fought in the Roman army.

The map, period by period

Pick a period, or drag the year slider to see the battles of any 25 years; Play runs through them from the founding of Rome. Each dot is a place, its area the number of battles there, and its slices the opponents, starting at 12 o'clock and running clockwise in the order of the legend; grey is civil war. Diamonds are sea battles; dashed rings are battles placed only within their region, in the colour of the most frequent opponent; triangles at the edge point to Caledonia, Nubia and Arabia. Nearby dots merge as you zoom out and split as you zoom in. Hover or tap a dot for its battles; select it to list them with their full records.

Wins, draws and losses

The Roman record in the 1,469 battles with a known result. The Roman states are counted together; the 236 civil-war battles and the 4 with unknown results are left out. A battle with several opponents counts against each of them, and once in the total. Select a row to list its battles.

The Empire includes the western and eastern courts of 395–480 and the restored empire of 1261–1453; the other successor states are counted on their own. Germans, Goths, Gauls and the Greek cities are many peoples or cities under one name; the note of each battle names them. Traditional battles are counted as the sources tell them: they are most of the early Republic's record, which modern historians doubt.

All the battles

Select a row for the full record: date, place, sides, commanders, the Roman army and its languages, the sources, and for doubtful entries the reason. Results give the winner first; the coloured edge marks a Roman win (blue), draw (grey) or defeat (orange). Traditional marks a battle known only from a tradition that modern historians doubt; borderline an entry that may not meet the rules, kept with the reason (hover the label to read it). A hollow marker means that only the region of the battle is known.

How it was made

What counts as a battle

A named land or sea battle in which a Roman force fought, as a principal belligerent or as a distinct allied force: one with its own name in the ancient and medieval historians or the standard modern works, or in English Wikipedia's battle lists and campaign boxes. Sieges count when there was real fighting (assaults, sorties, relief battles); a distinctly named battle inside a siege is its own entry. Left out: unnamed skirmishes, massacres without a battle, captures and surrenders without fighting, and legends. When in doubt an entry is kept and marked borderline, with the reason: 649 entries, most of them minor.

Who counts as Roman

The Roman Kingdom, the Republic and the Empire, West and East as one state (the western court to 480, the eastern throughout), and after 1204 every Roman successor state: Nicaea and the empire it restored at Constantinople (1261–1453), Epirus, Thessalonica (1224–46), Trebizond (1204–1461), the Morea (1349–1460) and Theodoro in the Crimea (to 1475). Client kings and allies (Pergamon, Rhodes, the Numidian kings, the Ghassanids, the federates) are not Rome; Romans in foreign service do not make a battle Roman.

Traditional battles

The battles of the kings and the early Republic are included as the ancient historians tell them, and 94 battles known only from a tradition that modern historians doubt are marked traditional, each with the reason. Most are the battles of 753–391 BCE: they rest on Livy, Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Plutarch, who wrote centuries later from the annalists, and Livy himself says that most records of early Rome perished when the Gauls burnt the city (6.1). Others are victories that Livy took from annalists who invented or inflated them (the Scipios in Spain, 215–214 BCE), and a few medieval battles known only from much later chronicles (the early Ottoman traditions of 1284–1303).

Civil wars

Battles with Roman forces on both sides, 236 in all, are mapped and listed, in grey, with the factions (Caesar and the Pompeians, Constantine and Maxentius, John VI Kantakouzenos and the regency), but not scored. Foreign armies in a Roman civil war count as allies. Palmyra under Zenobia is counted as an opponent; the battles of the Gallic emperors are civil wars.

Colours and dots

Each battle takes the colour of its main opponent: Greeks, Persians and the East, peoples of Italy (Latins, Etruscans, Samnites, the Italian allies, the slave revolts), Carthage and Africa (with Numidia, Mauretania and the Moors), Western Europeans, Balkan peoples and Slavs (Illyrians, Thracians, Dacians, Bulgaria, Serbia, the Rus'), steppe peoples, Seljuks and Ottomans, and Arabs (the caliphates and emirates, the Fatimids and Hamdanids); grey for civil wars. Each place is drawn as a small pie: its area grows with the number of battles there, and the slices give the number against each opponent.

Wins, draws and losses

The victor is the side that won on the battlefield: it held the field, routed the enemy, took the city or destroyed the fleet; strategic outcomes and disagreements between the sources are in the notes. In a siege the besieger wins if the place fell and the defender if the siege was raised. A draw is an indecisive battle, or one that both sides claimed with no clear verdict from modern scholarship.

The Roman armies

For every battle the research recorded who fought on the Roman side: the contingents as the sources give them (legions and allies, themes and tagmata, federates and mercenaries), their main languages, and an estimate of the share of Greek speakers, with its basis and a confidence. Where the sources say nothing, the estimate rests on where that army was recruited in that period, and the record says so: 1,061 of the 1,709 estimates are of low confidence.

Research

Twenty-one research batches by period, each following the same written rules and citing its sources battle by battle; then twelve coverage and fact-check passes against the Wikipedia lists and war articles, which added 123 battles, corrected 129 fields and removed 7 entries (listed under Left out); then checks of names, of possible doubles and of every position against the present-day country. Positions: 586 known, 812 approximate, 311 only within their region.

The images

The 18 images were made for X in the house style of the series, 3200 × 1800 pixels: the overview map with the number of battles per 20 years, Italy with Rome and Latium, the Balkans, Anatolia and the East with Constantinople, the win-draw-loss tables, and the list in fourteen parts.

Data

Every battle with its date, war, place, coordinates, sides, allies, commanders, result, the Roman state, the Roman army (contingents, languages, an estimate of the share of Greek speakers, with its basis and confidence), the reasons for doubt, sources and notes: as a CSV table, as JSON, or as a spreadsheet with the record tables and the entries left out; and the record alone as CSV.

Sources, cited battle by battle: Livy, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Polybius, Plutarch, Appian, Caesar, Cassius Dio, Tacitus, Josephus, Ammianus Marcellinus, Zosimus, Procopius, Agathias, Theophylact Simocatta, Theophanes, Leo the Deacon, Skylitzes, Attaleiates, Anna Komnene, Kinnamos, Choniates, Akropolites, Pachymeres, Gregoras, Kantakouzenos, Sphrantzes, Chalkokondyles, Panaretos and the Arabic and Ottoman chroniclers, among others; for what counts as a battle, the battle lists, war articles and campaign boxes of English Wikipedia and the standard modern works (the Cambridge Ancient History, Goldsworthy, Haldon, Treadgold, Bartusis, the Oxford Dictionary of Late Antiquity and Kazhdan's Oxford dictionary of the medieval empire). Map: Natural Earth; today's borders dashed.
