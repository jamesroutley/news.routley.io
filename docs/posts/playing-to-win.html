Playing to win

November 22, 2026

As I’ve written about before, every Monday night at 9pm I play pickup soccer with the same group of guys. The games and the gameplay are always different — it depends on who’s there, what the teams are, and who’s “on” that night.

When the other team has the ball, there are two main tactics. The safe option is to defend your half of the field — let them carry the ball to the halfway line before you start pressing. The more aggressive approach is a “high press”, where you try to win the ball back in the other team’s end. If you succeed, you’ve got the ball near your opponents’ goal, with most of their players out of position. If you fail, you let the other team attack while you’re out of position.

I like to play the high press. When it works, it’s just so much fun.

But I’ve noticed something — sometimes we’ll be playing this way, and it’s clearly working. We’ll be up 2-0, then 4-2, and then 6-3…but at some point, after the other team scores, someone starts yelling: “C’mon guys! Let’s tighten up the defense!”

It’s so funny to me. Our strategy is working, we’re winning, and they want us to do something different?

Kent Beck, in the first edition of Extreme Programming Explained:

It was always wonderful to watch John Wooden’s UCLA basketball teams. They were usually crushing the opposition. However, even if the game was close going into the final minutes, UCLA was absolutely certain that they were going to win. After all, they had won so many, many times before. So, they were relaxed. They did what they needed to do. And they won again.

The difference is between playing to win and playing not to lose. Most software development I see is played not to lose. Lots of paper gets written. Lots of meetings are held. Everyone is trying to develop “by the book,” not because it makes any particular sense, but because they want to be able to say at the end that it wasn’t their fault, they were following the process.

Playing not to lose. Once you’ve heard it, you start seeing it everywhere.

Obviously it happens a lot at big companies…no one ever got fired for buying IBM and all that. Public policy too — it often seems like people want to be seen doing something, more than they actually want to solve the problem.

But I think it’s deeper than that. No one at my pickup soccer game is “managing optics” or trying to cover their ass. I think it’s just a cognitive shortcut: all will be well if we can just prevent bad things from happening.

Does it work? Maybe, sometimes.

I happen to think playing to win is more effective. It’s certainly harder: you need the courage to put yourself at risk, to go against conventional wisdom, and maybe look dumb in the process.

But it’s also a hell of a lot more fun.
