I asked every TV manufacturer 17 questions about how they spy on you

Every TV company is spying on you, not just LG. Like other tech companies, they collect lots of information, and while some is necessary for products to work, not all of it is — and could include details you’d prefer to keep private. Many customers are concerned about how TVs and streaming devices might be spying: listening to what you say, taking screenshots of everything you watch, storing sensitive information on your TV, and sharing your data with the cloud.

To find out what TV and streaming device companies are doing, I decided to ask every one to explain itself. I put together 17 yes-or-no questions, like “Do your TVs store any recorded audio, or transcripts based on audio, captured in the home?” so that companies couldn’t just link to a confusing and legalese-ridden privacy policy. TV users deserve to know the answers.

LG Electronics: Chris De Maria, director of public relations

Roku: Sarah Novatt, spokesperson

One month ago, I invited 11 companies, including Amazon, Apple, Google, Hisense, LG, Panasonic, Roku, Samsung, Sony, TCL, and Walmart’s Vizio to share those answers. We now have answers from the top three TV manufacturers as well as every major streaming device company. Some companies declined to answer. Below, you can see and compare how each company answered (or failed to answer) each question and then decide how you feel. (One company, Hisense, ignored our emails entirely.)

As you’ll see them say, some of this data collection is opt-in, and you can often opt out. We know that many TVs use automatic content recognition (ACR) to figure out potentially everything we’re watching, and use that information to tailor ads, gather audience metrics, and serve up content recommendations based on our viewing habits. But though it sometimes seems like it’s required during the cavalcade of agreements thrown at us during setup, you can generally stop ACR even after you’ve agreed to it.

Opting out might not be as effective as it should be, however. A recent Northeastern University study found that Walmart’s Vizio kept on sending data after an opt-out (Vizio told me it was “routine operational data used to support the Vizio experience,” not ACR), while traffic with both Samsung and Roku was limited, but not stopped completely.

Below are the answers to our 17 questions. You will notice that some companies would not answer yes or no. We’ve also noted where companies declined to answer at all or ignored the questions. If any company becomes willing to directly answer our questions, we’ll add their responses.

1. Do your TVs ever use their embedded microphones, or the microphones in their remotes, to listen to sound in a customer’s home?

Amazon:

No. Fire TV includes voice functionality to help customers use their devices and get to content they love and fast, not to surveil homes. The microphones on our voice remotes are designed to only work when the mic button on the remote is physically pressed. And TVs with hands-free Alexa are designed to detect the wake word (and only the wake word) before connecting to Alexa. No audio is stored or sent to the cloud unless the device detects the wake word. Customers can also turn off the microphones at any time. Every Fire TV smart television that has microphones also has a physical button or switch that disables the microphone.

Apple:

Apple TV 4K will use its far-field microphones and the Always On Processor strictly to listen for the Siri wake word. The Always On Processor does not process or act on any other sounds in the home. Users have full control over this and can turn off “Siri” or “Hey Siri” as a wake word, or disable Siri entirely. When using the Siri Remote, users must actively press and hold the microphone button for it to start listening.

Google:

If Google Assistant or Gemini is enabled or available, microphones (built-in hands-free ‘Hey Google’ or remote push-to-talk button) listen for activation triggers. Microphones remain in passive standby until triggered by hotword or button press.

3. Do your TVs transmit any audio, or transcripts based on audio, captured in the home?

Amazon:

When customers speak to Alexa by pressing the mic button on the remote or using their chosen wake word, a recording of the request is sent to Amazon’s cloud, where a text transcript of the request is created to help Alexa provide a response. No audio is captured without the customer invoking Alexa—either by pressing the mic button or saying the wake word—and customers can easily review exactly what they asked and what Alexa heard, delete their recording, or even choose not to save them at all.

Apple:

Siri AI processes user requests using on-device processing, and when a request needs more powerful AI models, it extends those same on-device privacy protections to the cloud through a groundbreaking infrastructure called Private Cloud Compute (PCC). When Private Cloud Compute is handling users’ requests, their personal data is not stored nor made accessible to Apple, or anyone else, and outside experts can verify this privacy promise at any time.

For moments when users ask a question that requires up‑to‑date information, Siri AI can go out to the web and find it, then use Private Cloud Compute to generate an answer. Searches and requests are associated with a random identifier — a long string of letters and numbers — not a user’s Apple Account.

Google:

If Voice & Audio Activity is turned on, when voice features are activated (via hotword or remote button), captured audio or transcripts are transmitted to Google cloud servers for processing and fulfilling commands. If VAA is turned off, these are deleted.
