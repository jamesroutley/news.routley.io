MONOPOLY PROPERTY NUMBER 13: FLEET STREET

(Details of books and maps at the end of the post)

Fleet Street in and of itself is a short road, potentially making this a fleeting (haha) visit. However, if you care to explore legal London then the roads running off Fleet Street lead you to the four historic Inns of Court, the Law Society and the Royal Courts of Justice. You can spot these institutions in the map below.

 

 

The four Inns of Court (Gray’s Inn, Lincoln’s Inn, Middle Temple and Inner Temple) are the professional associations for barristers. During my training, all students had to dine 24 times before qualifying (after passing the Bar Exams) as a Barrister. It was a particular custom of Gray’s Inn that the most junior member of hall (i.e. the person sitting in the last seat on the outermost table, closest to the door) had to ask for permission to smoke at the end of dinner.

A smoking ban in England, making it illegal to smoke in all enclosed public spaces and workplaces in England, came into force only in July 2007.

Requesting for permission to smoke was quite farcical. The most senior member of hall would pretend not to hear and when they eventually decided to entertain the request, it was only on condition that the student sing a song, recite a poem or perhaps even do some acrobatic tricks. My memories are hazy on purpose, and I must add that I never once arrived so late as to be in that junior seat. In fact I make it a point never to arrive late for any function, and maybe that was the point of the exercise – to always be in time for a court session to avoid being humiliated.

 

HOW TO GET HERE

For transport options, Citymapper is invaluable as a tool to get from A to B. I visited Fleet Street when I went to see my dentist in Chancery Lane, taking the tube there.

As a matter if interest, smoking was banned inside all London Underground train carriages from July 1984. Smoking was still permitted on some platforms, however in November 1987 a complete ban on smoking throughout all areas of the underground was enforced following the King’s Cross fire.

Just outside Temple Church is a bronze sculpture atop a stone column. It depicts two knights sharing a single horse, symbolising the early Knights Templar’s vow of poverty, as they were initially too poor to afford a separate horse for each knight. The 10-meter column marks the exact spot where the Great Fire of London was extinguished in 1666.
