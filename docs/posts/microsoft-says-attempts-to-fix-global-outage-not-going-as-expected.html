  • Company reliance on one system makes outages like this a severe challengepublished at 10:17 GMT

    10:17 GMT

    Jaltson Akkanath Chummar

    Today’s disruption of Microsoft services highlights the risks faced by businesses that rely heavily on a single technology provider for major operation needs.

    Companies generally go for one particular tech company like Microsoft or Google for their email, meeting and software needs as it's easier to manage a single provider and is more cost effective.

    But that makes tech outages a more severe challenge for those businesses.

    "When several critical services depend on the same provider, one disruption can affect multiple business functions simultaneously. This is known as concentration risk," says Bernard Tan from global technology firm Protiviti.

    He says the issue isn't relying on one major provider, but whether "companies understand these dependencies and have practical alternatives when services become unavailable," Bernard told the BBC.

    "A more practical approach is to invest in alternative arrangements for the services that are most important to their operations," he added.

  • Microsoft: 'Early signs' of systems stabilising but system health remains variablepublished at 09:30 GMT

    09:30 GMT

    Microsoft has given another update as it works to fix the outage.

    It says it is seeing "early signs of stabilisation", but service capacity has "not yet returned to normal levels".

    System health "remains variable" it says, as traffic is "carefully reintroduced", it says.

    "As part of this effort, we are extending the email timeout window to 48 hours to help protect delayed messages while service recovery continues," the statement says.

    The email timeout window is, effectively, the amount of time a client like Outlook will spend waiting to receive an email.

    Microsoft is doing this to avoid a situation where emails which have been sent - such as from a Gmail account or something else unaffected - do not ultimately arrive when things are fixed.

    Microsoft says it is continuing to "treat this with urgency", and will share a full report after the incident is resolved.

  • How many people are really affected?published at 09:15 GMT

    09:15 GMT

    Tom Gerken

    I’ve been writing about outages for years, and one problem we always come up against is a question of scale.

    Perhaps unsurprisingly, companies are rarely eager to tell us just how many people are affected when their products become inaccessible.

    So, we often rely on the website Downdetector, which tracks online outages. It said it received 43,000 reports of issues with Microsoft Outlook at around 07:00 GMT, but that number is typically a low estimate.

    For one thing, it’s only helpful for the countries where folk are awake - at its peak, there were more than 130,000 reports in the US, a number far lower now it's the middle of the night.

    And the other glaring issue is it only really tells us how many people use the website. Think of this - are you affected by the outage and did you tell Downdetector about it?

    I think for a lot of people, the answer to that question would be a “yes” followed by a “no”.

    With 430 million people using Office365 services according to Microsoft, you have to wonder just how many aren’t able to access their emails this morning.

    It all points to this being a massive, global outage.
