The Commodore 64 hardware simulator: break chips and pins, see the results

c64-hw-sim simulates the motherboard of the Commodore 64, ASSY 250407, pin by pin. You can lift the leg of a chip, short it to ground or to +5V, pull a chip out of its socket, or open a resistor. Then you watch what the machine does: the picture on the monitor, the keyboard, every signal on the board.

The program knows the board's netlist (the electrical connections on the PCB) and how each chip behaves at its pins. It knows nothing about what a broken Commodore 64 looks like: when you simulate a broken part and the screen fills with garbage, that garbage was computed from the circuit, not chosen by someone who knew what to expect.

Here is an example. Lift pin 17 (A7) of the color RAM before power on, and the machine boots and looks healthy. The CPU writes and the VIC reads with the same wrong address, so the RAM simply aliases. Put the leg back, and the half of the color RAM that was never written shows up as random colors. Lift it again, and the screen is clean. A real 2114 does the same.

With this program you can do mainly two things:

  1. Learn how the Commodore 64 works at a more intimate level, in a way that is hard to reproduce on real hardware without the risk of damaging it (and on most boards many chips are soldered, not socketed).
  2. If you have a broken C64 and some hypothesis about what could be broken, simulate it with this program and perhaps gain clues, so that you can desolder chips in the most logical order, and desolder as few of them as possible (desoldering is a boring process, and every time it risks damaging the board...). A matching symptom is a clue, not a proof: different faults can look the same, and the simulation has its limits (see below).

Please note that even on fast computers this program struggles to simulate the C64 hardware at real speed. A real C64 is simulated at 31% of its speed on an Apple M3 Max, 39% on an AMD Ryzen AI Max+ PRO 495 (Gorgon Halo), and 48% on an Apple M5 Max. This is not a problem for the goals of this program, but be warned: it is not an emulator to play games with.

Above: the classic 10 PRINT CHR$(205.5+RND(1));:GOTO 10 maze is running when the character ROM (U5) is pulled out of its socket, and later put back. The CPU, the RAM and the program keep working; only the shapes of the characters are lost, as repairers know well. The scope is on the data line D0 of the ROM socket: with the socket empty, the bus floats (the dotted segments) whenever the VIC fetches a character shape. Shown at C64 speed.

I (antirez) got the idea for this program while using and observing Andre Weissflog's (floooh) chips, emulators of the 6502, VIC-II, CIA, SID and friends that use pins as their interface. The next step was to take this approach, with his code as a starting point, to its logical extreme: pin-level models of every other part of the C64, the RAMs, the ROMs, the logic chips, the PLA, and so forth.

The chip cores are floooh's chips, taken from c64-llm (zlib license). The board comes from bwack's C64-250407-Replica-KiCad (CERN OHL). The PLA equations come from the 82S100 read-out by Mark Smith and Marko Mäkelä.

make
./c64-hw-sim [program.prg]

It runs on macOS and Linux (X11), with OpenGL. The graphics library (sokol) and the interface library (nuklear) are in third_party/.

The window shows the board, the monitor, a control panel and an oscilloscope.

Chips are boxes named by role:

  • RAM1 to RAM8 are the 4164 DRAMs. Each one holds a single bit of every byte: RAMn has data bit n-1.
  • VRAM is the 2114 color RAM.
  • BASIC, KERNAL and CHAR are the ROMs.
  • PLA is the address decoder.

Zoom in to see references and address ranges. Pins show their state as a logic probe would: red high, blue low, yellow pulsing, gray floating, magenta when two outputs fight.

Click a pin to put it on the oscilloscope and to break it: floating, shorted to GND, or shorted to +5V. Click a chip to pull it out. Click a resistor, a ferrite bead or the crystal to open it. The control panel lists the faults, each with a repair button.

The control panel also loads .prg files. They are written straight into the DRAM chips, as a freezer cartridge would do it, so loading works on a broken board too.

Type on your keyboard as usual. ESC is RUN/STOP, PgUp is RESTORE, TAB is CTRL, and left ALT is C=.

./c64-hw-sim --headless 200 --text --shot out.png --fault U17:removed

This runs four seconds (200 frames) without a window. It prints the text read from the monitor picture, as a person would read it, and saves the picture. A fault is written REF:what or REF.PIN:what:

U17:removed   U21.14:gnd   U12.14:vcc   U9.2:open   R51:open   RP4:short

Other options:

  • --at 150:U6.17:open --at 300:repair breaks and repairs parts while the C64 runs.
  • --nets reports the nets where outputs fought.
  • C64HW_TYPE=$'PRINT 1+1\n' types text after boot.
  • C64HW_CHECK=1 checks every KERNAL read and every VIC fetch against the ideal memory map.
  • --record 900:DIR --frames 90 (graphical mode) saves the window 90 times, every 4 emulated frames from frame 900 on. This is how the GIF above was made.

tests/faults.sh runs 31 common faults. The expected symptom of each one was written down before the simulator was run.

tools/kicad2c.py turns bwack's KiCad replica of the 250407 into C tables: 216 parts, their pads, and 249 nets.

Every pin drives its net strongly, weakly (a pull-up), from a supply short, or not at all. The strongest drive wins. Two equal drives fighting are a contention, and low wins.

A net that nobody drives keeps its charge, and then drifts:

  • to about 1.4V within microseconds, if TTL inputs load it;
  • toward 0V in a fraction of a second, if only MOS inputs are on it.

MOS inputs compare that voltage, plus some noise, with their own threshold, so a floating line can read erratically.

Power faults depend on the chip:

  • A chip without VCC is dead.
  • A chip without GND lets its outputs float high.
  • A CMOS chip keeps running on phantom power, which reaches it through the protection diodes of its pins.

The noise is seeded, so a run can be repeated exactly.

src/parts.c models these parts at their pins:

  • the glue logic;
  • the ROMs;
  • the DRAMs, which latch the row on /RAS and the column on /CAS;
  • the color RAM;
  • the PLA, with its real logic equations.

The 6510, VIC-II, CIAs and SID are cycle-accurate cores from floooh's chips. In src/board.c each core sits in a socket that sees only its pins. Each half cycle runs in three steps: the row address, then /RAS falls, then /CAS falls. The whole board settles after each step.

PLAN.md explains the reasoning behind these choices.

It is slow, between a third and half of real time on the computers listed above, because every pin of every chip is simulated.

The floating and power models have the right shape, but their numbers (thresholds, drift times, crosstalk) are guesses. They are constants in src/sim.c.

These analog parts are not simulated: the video output, the SID filter, the power supply, and the dot clock PLL.

Faults inside a chip, such as bad DRAM cells or slow parts, and bridges between two nets are planned but not done.
