17: Hans Moravec - a pioneering AI researcher, forecaster, and futurist - is considered one of the grandfathers of modern AI and singularitarianism - a figure of similar stature as Alan Turing or Isaac Asimov. So everyone in the field was recently surprised to learn that he was still alive. Dan Kagan-Kans hunted him down (paywalled): he is 77, lives in a retirement community in Pennsylvania, and, although his body struggles with myasthenia gravis, his mind remains sharp. He is happy to be recognized as a brilliant forecaster (in the 1990s, he predicted AGI in 2028, which now seems about right) but says he’s out of the game:

“I’m a spectator at this point,” he says. “I’m relaxing. It’s great to see AI progressing the way it is without me having to do any effort.” … “That’s what I do now. I have a conversation with my artificial friend,” he says of ChatGPT. What do they talk about? His old hobby passions — “nuclear-power ideas, starship designs” — and the current trajectory of AI, which he confirms is proceeding to plan. “We’re on track, more or less. It’s not going to take that long,” he says.

As for his p(doom), he says that “If I had to put probabilities on it, 50/50 oblivion. And then the other 50 percent — the wildest thing you can imagine.”

20: AI Futures Project on how the US could “pace the frontier” of AI. Suppose the US has a six month lead over China. That means they have the option to pause for 5.99 months (and use that time to focus on safety) without China breaking ahead. The conventional wisdom is that if they’re going to do this, they should do it at the last possible moment, so that they have the best possible AIs to help with their safety research. AIFP breaks with that and suggests pausing now. Why? US companies currently have such bad security that China can easily steal any advances we make; therefore, continuing to race buys us no extra lead (China’s continued handicap comes down to having less compute, which is harder to steal). We can spend the pause increasing our security along with safety research and other things, and then race later, if we have to (they continue to prefer a mutual pacing deal with Beijing).

Maybe relatedly, Anthropic claims that they “recently chose to delay pre-training a new frontier base model for more than a month to allow time to further enhance our security measures related to preventing model weight exfiltration”.

22: Bill Gates on climate, hopefully self-recommending. My summary:

  • If we’re against climate change because of how it will hurt poor people in developing countries, we need to take into account that giving those people standard health + development aid seems more cost-effective than most climate spending.

  • The most promising and cost-effective strategy for fighting climate change is accelerating development of environmentally-friendly technologies until they can outcompete dirty technologies on the open market, and so far this strategy is going well (most notably solar power, but also low-carbon cement and steel, and lots of other things most people don’t hear about).

  • In the past ten years, mostly through strategies like this, we’ve cut projected emissions by over 40% (!)

  • This isn’t a call to sit around doing nothing; incubating new green technologies that outcompete dirty technologies is hard, and we need to keep doing this hard thing.

24: Related: Jean Hardouin was a 17th century French priest who believed we only had about ten real works of ancient literature (including Homer, some Virgil, and some Cicero) - everything else was made up by a 13th century conspiracy, possibly led by Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II. Also, the history of the world between about 100 - 1500 AD was totally unknown, with the current version also made up by said conspiracy. He seems to have disagreed with some of the Church Fathers’ writings, which would have been heretical unless those writings were forgeries, and he solved the web of dependencies by concluding that almost all recorded history was a world-building project by Frederick meant to provide context for his forged incorrect pseudo-Church-Father tracts. He also thought that most Greek and Latin literature sucked, and felt like real Greeks and Romans could have done better.

25: Richard Hanania on the nature of the race and IQ taboo. He mostly focuses on an IMHO boring point about overlapping bell curves, but in the process briefly alludes to what seems to me like a much more interesting question. The classic story says that race science is taboo because it might lead to ordinary non-scientific racism where people use racial slurs and discriminate and commit hate crimes. But Hanania suggests that ordinary non-scientific racism is now less taboo than race science; it’s easier (on the Right) to say you think ethnic group XYZ is smelly and criminal and should be kept out of the country, than to say that maybe XYZs have such-and-such a gene which lowers their IQ by N points. Is this true, and, if so, how could it be possible? Maybe practical forms of racism (like directly ascribing negative qualities to certain immigrant groups) at least gets conservatives some of the things they want (less of those immigrant groups), which gives them a powerful set of defenders that race science doesn’t have?

35: Seth Hart on reincarnation research. This is often held up as one of the most convincing cases of paranormal phenomena, alongside UAPs and sun miracles. But Hart is unconvinced, and able to cite some very damning statistics. For example, did you know that the Tlingit believe reincarnation is matrilineal, the Igbo believe reincarnation is patrilineal, and both tribes’ compelling stories of reincarnated children match their cultural beliefs (rather than being globally consistent)? Or that the time between death and reincarnation (eg if a child claims to be X’s reincarnation, the time between X’s death and the child’s birth) also varies across cultures and is linked to the culture’s beliefs? And some of the most convincing cases - where children seem to know facts about their preincarnation that they “couldn’t possibly” have learned through natural means - involve children who were born before their preincarnation even died at all!

36: Kelsey looks into Alpha School. She concludes that at least one exciting claim - that they have 2x, 3x, or 7x faster learning than traditional school, is statistical malpractice. They have many other exciting claims that Kelsey doesn’t address, but if they’re faking one of them, odds that they’re faking the others go way up. However, her conclusion is more measured: Alpha is speedrunning traditional instruction so they have more time to do non-traditional “character-building” things; their speedrunning doesn’t produce miraculous results, but this is still a valid philosophy of education. Also:

“I love the school,” Naimoli wrote in a review that I would otherwise be inclined to call a scathing debunking of Alpha. “Alpha kids are increasing their MAP scores faster than kids at traditional schools, but the number is not 2.6 times faster. 2.6 is an erroneously computed and meaningless number.”

These sorts of testimonials put some of Alpha School’s negative press in a different light. Wired, for example, breathlessly reported on students who had a bad experience and left several years behind, dismayed by a school that set unreasonable expectations and deployed frustrating edtech. All of those things are bad. But they’re also all unfortunately and utterly normal.

It’s hard to imagine a Wired exposé of a specific local elementary school where only 20% of children learn to read. Alpha failing, in other words, is news, while the default system failing is not newsworthy at all.

40: OpenAI fired three safety researchers in September, originally without any explanation. Last week, the company finally gave a supposed reason - they claimed the researchers had leaked the information that OpenAI was using potentially-dangerous looped transformer models to AI safety researcher Ryan Greenblatt, who in turn had leaked it to tech industry news publication The Information. But this seems to be false; the only evidence OpenAI provided was that they talked to Greenblatt using secret-messaging-app Signal, but they were supposed to talk to Greenblatt as part of their jobs, and Signal is near-default in Silicon Valley. Also, Greenblatt denies it much more vociferously than guilty people usually deny things, and people who know him (including me) consider him pretty honest . So why did OpenAI fire the researchers? Daniel Kokotajlo’s theory is that maybe they are trying to discredit METR (which Ryan is associated with) as part of a campaign to avoid fulfilling their commitment to get external auditors (since METR is generally considered one of the most likely candidates for such an auditor). But then why did they make the commitment? Or why don’t they just hire Accenture to be their external auditor (Anthropic seems on track to use both Accenture and METR) and avoid METR entirely?

I would weakly guess that OpenAI panicked over the looped-transformer leak, asked someone who didn’t understand how Signal worked to play detective, and genuinely thought that the messages were damning. But it’s also possible they’re trying to cover up some other secret (though, if so, why haven’t the fired safety researchers gone public with the secret yet?)
