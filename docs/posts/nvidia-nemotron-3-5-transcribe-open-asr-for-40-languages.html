Nvidia Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe: open ASR for 40 languages

One open model for multilingual transcription, speaker attribution, translation, and audio Q&A, with top leaderboard results, plus the open Granary v2 data pipeline and fine-tuning recipes to adapt it.

A transcript can capture every spoken word and still leave users or applications wondering who said what, how to translate it, or how to format domain-specific jargon. Multilingual calls, fast-paced meetings, and medical dictation each need different output: a translation, speaker labels, or correctly spelled medical terms.

NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe is an open-weight, promptable model that turns recorded speech into useful text, now available on Hugging Face and ModelScope. It brings transcription, speaker attribution (diarization), translation, and audio question answering into one checkpoint: it transcribes 40 languages, translates 31 of them to or from English, and attributes speakers in 13. It handles recordings up to an hour long and labels up to eight speakers. Developers can guide output with natural language, choose where their audio is processed, and adapt recognition to domain vocabulary and accents.

Transcribe is first among open-weight models on the Hugging Face Open ASR Leaderboard for English and sits on Artificial Analysis’s accuracy–active-parameter frontier. For speaker attribution, it posts the lowest error rate on David AI’s 21-language diarization leaderboard and ranks first in VoiceArena’s 14-system comparison. Quantized versions are coming soon, so you can run Transcribe locally on your RTX Spark, DGX Spark, or Jetson AGX Thor—or scale up on Blackwell data-center GPUs.

Granary v2’s multilingual data (available on Hugging Face and ModelScope) and reusable curation pipeline, together with NeMo Speech fine-tuning tools, extend prompt-level control to training: developers can define the written form they need and adapt the model to the speech their applications encounter. In a medical case study, fine-tuning cut word error rate (WER) on held-out speakers by 45.8% relative to the best prompt.

The same checkpoint can turn one meeting recording into different outputs, depending on the instruction:

  • “Transcribe this recording and label each speaker.”
  • “Translate this audio into English.”
  • “What decisions were made in this meeting?”

For unfamiliar terms, try context before training. In a healthcare setting, for example:

Transcribe this medical discussion in the original language. Possible terms: HbA1c, metformin. Use these terms only when supported by the audio. Preserve standard medical spelling and write numbers in conventional written form.

The model card has recommended presets, including reasoning for audio questions, plus the full prompt guide and a task-by-language matrix: language support differs by task, and audio question answering is trained in English. Word-level timestamps are also supported. As with any speech model, spot-check names, speaker changes, and answers against the recording.

Agora, whose real-time voice and video platform powers live conversations in apps, used word-level timestamps in a classroom demo: Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe turns a recorded class into a transcript shown alongside the replay, so students can see what was said and when. Among open-weight models, Transcribe leads on what was said; on who said it, it tops two speaker-attribution benchmarks outright.

Hugging Face Open ASR Leaderboard. The leaderboard measures transcription accuracy as word error rate (WER) on public test sets, in three tracks:

  1. English: first among open-weight models at 5.33% average WER (Figure 1).
  2. Multilingual: 4.62% mean WER across seven languages, the lowest among evaluated open-weight models in the model card’s Open ASR comparison.
  3. Long-form: 10.98% mean WER, also the lowest in that comparison.

Figure 1. Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe ranks first among open-weight models on the Hugging Face Open ASR Leaderboard (English) at 5.33% average WER, with API models excluded. Lower WER means fewer transcription errors.

Artificial Analysis Speech-to-Text Leaderboard. Artificial Analysis independently ranks speech-to-text models by AA-WER, its word error rate index. Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe ties for first among open-weight models at 2.8% AA-WER, with about 4B active parameters versus 24B for the model it ties. On AA-AgentTalk, the held-out voice-agent speech set that makes up 50% of the AA-WER index, Transcribe scores 1.8% WER, versus 2.3% for the next-best open-weight model. Transcribe pairs its speech encoder with a hybrid Mamba–Transformer mixture-of-experts language model, so only about 4B of its 30B parameters are active per token. That puts it on Artificial Analysis’s open-weight Pareto frontier of accuracy versus active parameters. On AA’s all-model chart, it ranks ahead of several widely used proprietary speech APIs.

Figure 2. Leading AA accuracy at about one-sixth the active parameters: Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe achieves 2.80% AA-WER with approximately 4B active parameters.

Speaker attribution across languages and overlap. Sortformer speaker cues reach the language model with the acoustic features, so Transcribe can tag each turn during generation instead of relying solely on a later step to attach speakers to words.

Specialized recordings can still call for adaptation to domain vocabulary and transcript conventions. Granary v2 and NeMo Speech fine-tuning address that, starting with the data behind Transcribe.

A promptable speech model requires training data with unambiguous formatting targets. Granary v2 is part of the Nemotron speech collection on Hugging Face and ModelScope. Built with NVIDIA NeMo Curator, its reusable pipeline prepared multilingual speech used in Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe’s training, producing aligned transcript variants, task labels, and inspectable quality metadata. The public manifest covers 463,062 hours across 48 languages and 16 source corpora, up from 25 languages in Granary v1. These figures describe the dataset release, not Transcribe’s full training mixture or its sampling weights.

Granary v2 generates three transcript forms for each recording (Table 1), the same verbatim and clean styles developers can request from Transcribe with a prompt. Beyond transcription, the pipeline produces short audio captions, code-switching restoration of loanwords, speech-grounded Q&A pairs, contextual ASR biasing terms, and translations with automatic quality scores.

Training target Illustrative transcript
Text-normalized, verbatim I paid, uh, twenty-five euros.
Inverse text normalization, with disfluencies I paid, uh, €25.
Inverse text normalization, without disfluencies I paid €25.

Table 1. Granary v2 training targets for the same utterance.

The pipeline is organized into four reusable stages:

  • 1. Audio Preparation (Metadata): Segments raw or long-form audio, tracks speakers across the full recording, and tags acoustic events and language. Granary v2’s sources were already segmented, so this release skipped this stage.
  • 2. Transcription (ASR): Routes each language to primary and recovery ASR models, falling back when the primary fails a hallucination check. Human reference transcripts help preserve trusted spellings while adding spoken forms and disfluencies.
  • 3. Task Label Generation (Text): Derives transcript variants, then checks character rate, hallucination patterns, and model or human-reference agreement. Only accepted records receive contextual-ASR terms and translations.
  • 4. High Fidelity (HiFi): Filters out segments that are noisy, clipped, or contain multiple speakers, keeping a smaller set of high-quality recordings for uses beyond ASR. For the VoxPopuli dataset, it reduced 354k hours to 111k hours across 22 European languages.

Figure 6. Granary v2 data curation pipeline, following one sample from raw audio through the Metadata, ASR, and Text stages to a training example.

Developers can run the NeMo Curator pipeline with Ray and NVIDIA Dynamo backends on their own recordings or authorized public corpora, or build on the Granary v2 public manifests. The manifests include the three transcript forms as tn_raw, itn_raw, and itn_clean, the labels described above, entity hints, derived instruction pairs, provenance, and a per-record quality flag. Download source audio separately under each corpus’s original license, and follow the release’s published terms.

Start with a development-set prompt that specifies language, script, and transcript format. In the case study below, naming English and the Latin alphabet in the prompt cut full-benchmark WER from 30.8% to 23.8%. For domain errors that remain, the NeMo Speech fine-tuning workflow pairs representative speech with explicit written targets.

The NeMo Speech medical ASR case study shows this progression on Eka Care’s open benchmark of recordings from Indian doctors and medical students, which has 3,619 clips and no designated training split. The study builds adaptation data from generated medical text and public medicine catalogs, then combines approximately 153 hours of synthetic speech, 29 hours of accented English from Common Voice, and 63 hours of medical recordings from MultiMed.

Figure 7. On the full test set of Eka Care’s Indian medical speech benchmark, a better prompt cuts WER from 30.8% to 23.8%, and fine-tuning brings it to 11.8%, about 62% fewer transcription errors than the model out of the box. The full set includes the 855 tuning clips used to choose the prompt and checkpoints; the held-out results below are the cleaner test.

Synthetic generation separates the written target from its spoken rendering: “HbA1c” is the label, while “H B A one C” is sent to the synthesizer. Multiple TTS voices broaden the audio; real recordings add accents and recording conditions. Training text is filtered for exact matches and shared eight-word sequences with benchmark references.

As with Granary v2’s explicit targets, training examples vary digits, unit spacing, company-name expansion, and brand-name hyphens, with instructions specifying the required convention. Some prompts include term lists with near-miss distractors, so the model learns to use a spelling only when the audio supports it. The final system uses one fixed convention prompt and one model pass, without retrieval or transcript post-processing.

The recipe trains the speech encoder and projection and uses LoRA to adapt the language model. Two runs use different data mixtures; after adapter merging, selected checkpoints are uniformly averaged into one final model. Checkpoint selection uses decoded development-set results, rather than training loss alone. The full adaptation, from text generation through evaluation, took about 14 GPU-hours on one NVIDIA GB300.

Domain gains, measured on held-out speakers

The study splits the benchmark by speaker into 855 tuning clips, used to choose the prompt and checkpoints, and 2,764 held-out clips, decoded once per final system. Using Eka Care’s KARMA scoring pipeline, fine-tuning cuts held-out WER from 22.44% for the best-prompt baseline to 12.17%, a 45.8% relative reduction and the clearest evidence that the gains generalize to new speakers. Keyword WER falls from 9.2% to 6.5%, and semantic WER from 10.0% to 7.6%. On the same held-out speakers and scorer, the adapted model’s 12.17% WER beats an open medical-specialist model (13.79%) and a widely used general-purpose ASR model (14.92%).

On general English, LibriSpeech test-clean WER rises slightly, from 1.54% to 1.69%. The medical specialist still leads on exact matches for brand-name drugs and diagnostics; the study points to real recordings of clinicians saying brand names as the next step. For your own domain, keep at least 100 real, labeled clips for checkpoint selection, and evaluate the entity types your application depends on alongside general-speech retention.
