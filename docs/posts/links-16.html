Links #16

It’s been five months since my last links post, which is nearly a lifetime in this strange, AI-accelerated world that we’ve woken up and found ourselves living in. I always try to share links with staying power instead of news-cycle items whose relevance is superseded by the next new thing. Despite that, I needed to revisit some of these links, just to make sure they were still useful. Everything below passed the test.

Communities of Not

Observations on what happens when a community builds it’s identity around being against something (think: atheism, child-free, anti-car, anti-vax, anti-ai, anti-abortion, etc). Regardless of the thing being opposed, what starts as mutual support tends to drift towards negativity and even harassment. He calls it a “trap” which feels like the perfect word to me. Better to define what you are for and find positive ways to work toward that goal.

We might have found aliens. Then we ran out of money.

You’d never guess from the clickbait-y title, but this 23-minute video was surprisingly great. It tells the story of “Sample 25,” a rock sample taken by NASA’s Mars Perseverance rover, that may hold evidence for life on Mars. The only problem: we have no way to bring it back to earth to confirm the discovery. I watched this with my kids and was super-impressed by the quality and pacing of the video. Great work to everyone involved.

AI-generated posters don’t have to be horrible

I’ve designed a bunch of event posters from scratch throughout my life, mostly for church and service activities. This kind of work is happening constantly, usually by non-professional volunteers, for an intended audience of 100 people or less. Naturally, I’ve seen a lot more AI-generated posters for these kinds of events recently (including many with the repetitive style the author mentions). Many things can be said about this trend, but this post focuses on one small thing: anybody making an AI-generated poster can significantly improve the result with a little bit of intention. I think that’s a message worth boosting.

Note: if you actually need to make a poster, check out this collection of sample prompts by the post’s author.

Why you, personally, should want a larger human population

About 10 years ago, I wrote a blog post similar to this one but I was afraid to publish it, so I just archived in in my notes (mine was called “On Overpopulation, Technology, and Sustainability”). Today, it’s less controversial to advocate for larger populations than it was a decade ago, but I’m sure it’s still a minority position. I feel like this post does a good job at making the arguments and I think more people should be exposed to them. For me, it’s all about how ambitious we want humanity to be. Personally, I want us to reach for the stars.

Jailbreaking a $50 photo frame

In this video, Wes Bos converts a cheap, $50 photo frame, into a home dashboard for his family, using nothing more than a computer, Claude Code, and some tokens. The project is neat, but even better is the fact that AI makes this kind of hardware-hacking accessible to anybody who is fairly tech savvy. It makes me want to get my kids involved in using AI hackery to revive old Kindles, build silly dashboards, and take ownership over the devices we have floating around the house. Of all the links I’m sharing today, this is the one that my brain can’t seem to stop thinking about.

AI is removing the middle class of software engineering

A better title for this might be “the battle for code quality in the age of AI.” All of the points center around this idea that shipping code we don’t understand will lead us to a world of unmaintainable software. It’s almost like human comprehension represents a simplicity bar that all AI-generated code needs to clear, or be thrown away. It reminds me of the times I’ve worked with brilliant coworkers in the past, who ended up committing brilliant code that became a liability because it was difficult for others to understand and maintain. In those times, I’ve seen my inability to digest complexity as an asset, since it forced me to produce simple code.

The post’s author would probably agree with my mantra, “you can’t fix a problem you don’t understand”. I used to believe this statement was a universal truth, but frontier AI models have become really good at fixing things (at least, some categories of things). I think the statement will survive, but we’ll need to adjust our definitions. What constitutes a “problem” will change, as will what it means to “understand” the solution.

Economics and AI take off

Casey Handmer discusses the fundamental forces causing some products to get cheaper and others to get more expensive (see: that classic Price Changes graphic). A few key concepts like “rivalrous goods,” and “saturable demand” go a long way to both explaining recent inflation and predicting how future prices are going to be impacted by the proliferation of AI. Everything here felt super-valuable for building a solid mental model of our financial system. The final paragraph gives a sample that I think everyone can agree with:

“The goal of policy should be to help our civilization ingest ongoing technological improvements without creating an artificial class of “have nots” who miss out, especially when there is so much wealth creation to go around and so little standing in the way of this rising tide lifting all boats.”
