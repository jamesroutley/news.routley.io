Tacit Knowledge, Company Brains, and AI

I’ve been getting a lot of cold emails from folks who think that they can set up a company to extract tacit knowledge and somehow this would lead to a valuable AI startup. Sometimes, very rarely, I tell them to go read everything in Commoncog’s Tacit Knowledge Series and run a simple Cognitive Task Analysis on someone senior they know before coming back to me. Absolutely nobody has done this.

As a result, you would think that I would be very outspoken about how attempts to extract tacit knowledge for AI are stupid. But actually I’ve been rather circumspect about this. I think AI might be able to help for certain types of tacit knowledge. Here’s why.

A friend of mine used to work in a major trading firm. He was an SRE, or Site Reliability Engineer, meaning he was basically a highly paid sysadmin (I’m joking, the modern job of keeping complex software systems running deserves to be highly paid; broadly, however, this is what the tech industry has decided to call the role). This firm had a system where — after a production incident was resolved — you could hit a button in Slack and then type out (or dictate) what the solution was. The message would go to an agent that would pull together relevant logs and metrics around the incident, attach your note to it, and then save it into a vector store.

This was during the Opus 4.5 era, where the models were good but not great; they would still regularly hallucinate file paths. As you might imagine this system didn’t work so well. I assume it works much better now.

The intended use case was this: all future incidents would invoke a triage agent, concurrently, whilst a human was paged. As part of its job, the triage agent would search the vector store of past incidents, in addition to poking around the various systems (it had read-only access to production systems, natch). So when the on-call human finally arrived, the agent would have some preliminary findings for them to read.

More importantly, the SRE could engage the agent in follow-up conversation whilst they sensemake the incident.

You could say that this is tacit knowledge — after all, knowledge of such incidents would otherwise only live in the heads of the engineers who were present for that incident. In the past, such tacit knowledge would’ve been preserved and distributed by doing something like ‘anecdote circles’, or even internal podcasts, or internal newsletters (see here for real examples from the chemical processing industry).

Perhaps the most famous story about tacit knowledge development and distribution is the 1996 book Talking About Machines: An Ethnography of a Modern Job by Julian Orr, about Xerox photocopier repair technicians.
