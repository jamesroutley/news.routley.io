MONOPOLY PROPERTY NUMBER 12: STRAND

(Details of books and maps at the end of the post)

Just one word: Strand. No definite article and nothing to indicate if it is a lane, street, road or avenue. A bit of history from No Free Parking:

The one street that can claim to be the start of the continuous history of London as a trading entrepôt is in neither the City of London nor Westminster. It is the street that links them: the Strand. The clue to its history, and physically why London’s story restarted here, lies in the name. For ‘strand’ is an Anglo-Saxon word which means shore or beach (as it still does in German). And it was on this northern Thames shore, sloping steeply upwards to safe, dry land from the Thames, that Anglo-Saxon traders or settlers pulled up their boats.

They could not do it to the south, for the land was low-lying and marshy, and they could not do it to the immediate east, into the old Roman city, for the wharfs had been blocked by a late Roman river-facing wall, now collapsing lumpishly into the river. Even if they could, old Roman waterfronts did not suit shallow Saxon vessels. But their ships could travel right up to this shore, below the old Roman road, on an incoming tide. So this is what they did and it was where London was reborn.

When referring to Strand in writing, most people add a definite article (the) and this is what I will do in this post.

 

 

On the map below we can see that Monopoly property no 12, Strand, is in the middle. Fleet Street (property number 13) lies to the east and Trafalgar Square (property number 14) is to the west. I am following the order as laid out on the board when visiting the red properties.

 

 

The river no longer runs up to the Strand. The riverbank was pushed back between 1865 and 1870 during the construction of the Victoria Embankment (to embank means to build a wall or a mound of soil and stone to stop water from coming inland). This was a massive public project led by civil engineer Sir Joseph Bazalgette to reclaim land from the river, primarily to provide London with a modern sewerage system. A low-level interceptor sewer was built directly below the embankment to stop raw sewage from flowing into the River Thames.

HOW TO GET HERE

For transport options, Citymapper is invaluable as a tool to get from A to B. I made several visits and always started from Charing Cross tube station, making my way along the length of the Strand eastwards either on foot or by bus. It is a relatively tidy but very busy street. Nothing has changed. One of the reasons for building the Victoria Embankment was to relieve traffic congestion on the Strand and Fleet Street.

Thinking about this, going back 160 years there would be no cars. Public transport looked very different, with road congestion caused by horse-drawn carriages. Today, cars ply the Victoria Embankment with the District and Circle lines running directly beneath.

 

ASPECTS OF MY VISIT

I read Law at the London School of Economics, and took a walk around my old campus. The whole area has evolved, there are grand new buildings and the narrow streets still teem with international students. In 1985 when I started at the LSE, the percentage of overseas students was around 40%. Today, the figure is around 64%. If you are a student from any country in the world, you should feel at home here.
