cyberwitchery

I’m starting a company. It’s called cyberwitchery, and it’s a small product and consulting studio. For now, it’s just two people: Erik Svedäng and me.

If you’ve read this blog for a while, you might know Erik as the author of Carp and the person I’ve been bugging with questions for the better part of a decade. Now I get to bug him professionally, which is nice. At cyberwitchery, he currently mostly works on alembic, which I’ll get to in a second, and a bit of metacarp.

Some of you have already seen the name cyberwitchery. It was on my slides this summer, it’s in the URL of metacarp, and I promised a sizable announcement in a post a few weeks ago.

This is that announcement, so let’s have a look around!

What we sell

We have a few products, two of which are public right now.

The first is alembic. It moves infrastructure data between systems like NetBox, Grafana, Arista AVD, and Ansible, through a vendor-neutral data model you describe yourself. The core and the inventory adapters are open source, and there is a commercial layer on top for driving labs, DNS, and the rest of the operational tooling and for integrating with your CI/CD pipelines and your chat.

The second is divinate. It collects the technical evidence behind security reviews and compliance work, and derives claims from that evidence. It is meant to run continuously, to guard things from drifting and to make sure your guarantees always match reality. It’s still early and doesn’t have a commercial story, but it’s out there.

Others are not open source, and they remain under wraps for now.

Next to the products, we do consulting, talks, and workshops: network automation, security, technical due diligence, and languages and developer tooling. If you’ve hired me in the last six years, none of this will surprise you. It’s more or less what I offered independently, with hopefully less key-person risk and a prettier invoice. The prices, too, stay comparable.

Why bother

So why change anything, if the work is mostly the same?

The first reason is a bit selfish. For years, my professional work and my private technical interests lived in the same place: my name, my GitHub account, my blog. That’s fine when you’re one person, but it got harder to tell where the consultant ends and the guy who builds arcane, weird shit in his evenings begins, both for me and, I think, for the people hiring me. With cyberwitchery, the professional half has a home of its own, and I get to be less judicious about what I put on here, not that I ever paid it much mind (that much should be obvious).

And of course I violated that principle immediately by making metacarp a cyberwitchery project. I’m only human, and a company that makes developer tools can reasonably own a compiler, I’d argue. Also, I wrote “we build hard software” on the website, so we better bring receipts.

The second reason is scope. As one person, I can only take on projects that fit into one person’s calendar. With two of us, and a company to put things under, we can do bigger and broader things, both for clients and for ourselves. I also have people I can trust to collaborate with, I can share the burden of my projects, maybe even take a vacation1.

Just because

The “do bigger and broader things for ourselves” part is what I’m most excited about, if I’m being honest.

There is a paper series, for instance. It’s short write-ups of problems we find interesting, published as PDFs that look like papers. They’re not peer reviewed, and they’re not always about anything we sell. The first one is about a trolley game in which your choices are remembered, the second about a drawing machine that tries to learn my taste, the third about metacarp.

And there’s merch! I’ve always been too cheap to do cool stuff with my own money, but if it’s company money it’s totally different, right?

As a small taster, we printed postcards with generative art on the front, five designs in total. Each of them puts the logo in the middle of a different algorithm: streamlines, tiles, circles, particles, and lines. The back has the algorithm itself, condensed into a few lines of pseudocode, so you can draw your own if you want to2.

Not strictly necessary for a consultancy, but it’s good for the soul. I think a small company without external investors to answer to should be allowed to be weird, and I’d rather spend some of our time on things like these than pretend we only care about billable hours. If you do want to pay for some art hours, though, I won’t say no, of course. Otherwise, just approach me at one of the upcoming conferences to get your very own artwork!

There is more to come. Starting cyberwitchery opened a door that I can’t seem to close anymore, so now I’m thinking about clothing that doesn’t suck, affordable full size art prints, and a book I’ve been writing. Not all on the same timeline, naturally3.

Fin

So that’s cyberwitchery: two people, a handful of products, consulting, and a growing pile of things we’re making because they seem cool and we like cool shit. If you want to work with us, book a call or write to contact@cyberwitchery.com. If you just want a postcard, ask me the next time you see me.

This blog isn’t going anywhere, just to be clear, and neither are my personal projects. cyberwitchery has its own log and GitHub org for the professional things, and I’ll keep building and writing about whatever I want here.

That’s all, folks! See you around!
