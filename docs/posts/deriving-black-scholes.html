Deriving Black-Scholes

Even if it feels unintuitive, the price of an option does not depend on whether you expect the underlying to moon or tank. Under some reasonable market hypotheses, we can build a fully hedged portfolio by holding the underlying and shorting the option. Since it is riskless, by no-arbitrage assumption, it must return the same as the risk-free interest rate. That allows to put together an equation (the Black-Scholes equation), and solve for the option price (the Black-Scholes formula).

Model §

A European call gives the right, but not the obligation, to buy an underlying at a fixed price K ⁣K\!K, the strike, on a fixed date T ⁣T\!T, the maturity. Its value V ⁣V\!V is the premium you need to pay for having that optionality, and it depends on time and on the underlying stock price S ⁣S\!S. At maturity it is the payoff1 1 For example, pay $5 today for the right to buy one AMZN share for $200 in a month. If AMZN then trades at $230, you buy at $200 and the option is worth $30. If it trades at $180, you walk away and the option is worth nothing. 

V(T,S)=max⁡(S−K,0). V(T,S)=\max(S-K,0). V(T,S)=max(S−K,0).

Stock moves scale with price, so it is common to model the returns instead of the price. Over a short time the return is a small drift plus noise. The noise is the sum of many small, unpredictable2 2 Weak-form efficient market hypothesis: past prices do not predict future moves.  sources. Such sum can be modeled as a Brownian motion W ⁣W\!W,3 3 By the central limit theorem, the sum of many small independent variables is normal.  so the return is

dSS=μ dt+σ dW ⁣, \frac{dS}{S}=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW\!, SdS​=μdt+σdW,

with drift μ\muμ and volatility σ ⁣\sigma\!σ. For our model, we will assume constant drift, volatility and interest rate r ⁣r\!r. We will also assume “perfect” trading, with fractional shares allowed, no fees, no taxes, no dividends, and no arbitrage.

Hedged portfolio §

The trick of Black-Scholes is to build a fully hedged portfolio combining the option and the underlying in the right proportion. By the no-arbitrage hypothesis, its returns must be the same as the interest rate r ⁣r\!r, and that way we can put together an equation.

When the stock moves a little, the option moves by Δ\DeltaΔ times as much, with

Δ=∂V∂S. \Delta=\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}. Δ=∂S∂V​.

If you short one option and hold Δ\DeltaΔ shares, then the two moves cancel. This is a delta hedge.

Note that Δ\DeltaΔ changes with the stock price and time. The portfolio is riskless only over an instant, and stays so only if it is rebalanced continuously.

Black-Scholes equation §

Use the Taylor expansion of the option value and keep the terms up to order dtdtdt. Since (dW)2=dt(dW)^2=dt(dW)2=dt, the (dS)2(dS)^2(dS)2 term is one of them,

dV=(μS∂V∂S+∂V∂t+12σ2S2∂2V∂S2)dt+σS∂V∂S dW. dV=\left(\mu S\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} +\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}\right)dt +\sigma S\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,dW. dV=(μS∂S∂V​+∂t∂V​+21​σ2S2∂S2∂2V​)dt+σS∂S∂V​dW.

Now, we can use the same hedged portfolio Π\PiΠ as before, short one option and long Δ\DeltaΔ shares, which is worth −V+ΔS ⁣-V+\Delta S\!−V+ΔS. Holding Δ\DeltaΔ fixed over an instant,

dΠ=−dV+ΔdS, d\Pi=-dV+\Delta dS, dΠ=−dV+ΔdS,

and substituting dSdSdS and dV ⁣dV\!dV gives

dΠ=(−∂V∂t−12σ2S2∂2V∂S2)dt. d\Pi=\left(-\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} -\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}\right)dt. dΠ=(−∂t∂V​−21​σ2S2∂S2∂2V​)dt.

See how noise and drift cancel. No dW ⁣dW\!dW term is left, and everything else is known at time ttt, so over the next instant the portfolio change is fixed.4 4 A known return with no randomness is precisely what riskless means.  We’ve built a riskless portfolio, that by the no-arbitrage assumption, it must earn the risk-free rate, dΠ=rΠ dt.d\Pi=r\Pi\,dt.dΠ=rΠdt. Equating both expressions gives us the Black-Scholes equation

∂V∂t+rS∂V∂S+12σ2S2∂2V∂S2=rV. \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}+rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} +\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}=rV. ∂t∂V​+rS∂S∂V​+21​σ2S2∂S2∂2V​=rV.

Note that μ\muμ is gone, so the option price does not depend on the drift.

τ=T−t,x=ln⁡S+(r−12σ2)τ,V(t,S)=e−rτw(τ,x), \tau=T-t,\quad x=\ln S+\left(r-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)\tau,\quad V(t,S)=e^{-r\tau}w(\tau,x), τ=T−t,x=lnS+(r−21​σ2)τ,V(t,S)=e−rτw(τ,x),

w(τ,x)=12πσ2τ∫−∞∞max⁡(ey−K,0) exp⁡(−(y−x)22σ2τ)dy. w(\tau,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2\tau}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \max(e^y-K,0)\,\exp\left(-\frac{(y-x)^2}{2\sigma^2\tau}\right)dy. w(τ,x)=2πσ2τ​1​∫−∞∞​max(ey−K,0)exp(−2σ2τ(y−x)2​)dy.

d1=ln⁡(S/K)+(r+12σ2)τστ,d2=d1−στ. d_1=\frac{\ln(S/K)+\left(r+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}, \qquad d_2=d_1-\sigma\sqrt{\tau}. d1​=στ​ln(S/K)+(r+21​σ2)τ​,d2​=d1​−στ​.
