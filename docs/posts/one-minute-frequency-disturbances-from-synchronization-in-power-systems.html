One-minute frequency disturbances from synchronization in power systems

Main

The transition to a sustainable energy system requires advanced control methods to deal with increasing complexity and uncertainty1,2. As variable renewable energy sources such as wind and solar replace conventional generation, maintaining power balance requires accurate forecasting and real-time control of storage, backup generation and flexible demand3. At the same time, the electrification of sectors such as transport and heating is introducing millions of digitally controlled devices—electric vehicle chargers4, heat pumps5 and smart appliances6—into the grid. Furthermore, data centres add additional software-dependent loads7. Together, these trends are reshaping the power system into a cyber–physical infrastructure, where physical devices and digital control are deeply intertwined. In this new paradigm, system reliability increasingly depends not only on the physical properties of generation and load but also on the design, timing and coordination of software-controlled devices across all layers of operation8.

While cyber–physical integration enables unprecedented levels of efficiency and flexibility, it also introduces new systemic risks9. Historical events underscore the stakes: inadequate situational awareness contributed to the 2003 North American blackout10, and software-driven control failures have played critical roles in recent major outages11,12. Beyond failures and malicious attacks, however, even benign control routines can become systemically relevant when many devices act coherently. Unintended synchronization can generate recurrent and potentially amplified oscillatory behaviour in large-scale cyber–physical energy systems.

Modern power systems exhibit oscillations across a broad range of time scales. Oscillations with characteristic periods around 1 min have previously been discussed as ultralow-frequency oscillations (ULFOs) and linked to resonance phenomena in hydropower turbine-governor systems13,14,15,16. However, resonance phenomena in classical control systems differ fundamentally from cyber–physical synchronization effects. Oscillations without external timing signals do not naturally explain patterns that remain phase-coherent over long time scales or occur reproducibly across diverse power systems.

In this Article, we identify and explain a coherent 1-min oscillation in power systems worldwide caused by cyber–physical synchronization effects. We show that the grid frequency, and thus the power balance, exhibits a highly regular periodic pattern with a period of exactly 1 min. Searching for potential causes of this pattern, we provide empirical evidence that clock-synchronized control actions in battery-based uninterruptible power supplies (UPSs) generate periodic power-demand peaks, and we demonstrate that this signal propagates across multiple voltage levels up to the transmission interface. More broadly, our results point to a class of cyber–physical power oscillations driven by externally synchronized control software.

The identified synchronization mechanism is not specific to UPSs or battery systems, but reflects a general property of digitally controlled devices operating with external timing signals or periodic software routines. The rapid expansion of battery storage, data-centre infrastructure and Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices increases the relevance of cyber–physical risks in power-system operation. At the same time, decreasing system inertia makes power systems more sensitive to coherent power imbalances17. Indeed, we observe that the amplitude of the 1-min pattern has increased in several grids. The oscillation contributes to wear and tear, especially in hydropower turbines18, and has motivated stricter load–frequency control requirements19. Our results indicate that coherent cyber–physical oscillations can be mitigated directly at their source through improved control design.

A universal 1-min pattern in the grid frequency

The grid frequency is a key observable in power-system operation, reflecting the balance between generation and load: it rises when generation exceeds demand and falls when demand outpaces supply. Maintaining the nominal frequency of 50 Hz or 60 Hz therefore requires continuous balancing by load–frequency control systems20.

Large frequency deviations occur during contingencies and severe disturbances and can endanger system stability21,22. Smaller but recurrent deviations arise continuously during normal operation through both stochastic fluctuations and deterministic mechanisms23,24.

To identify dominant recurrent patterns, we carry out a Fourier analysis of grid-frequency time series f(t) from multiple power systems worldwide (Fig. 1). The investigated grids span five continents and range in size from approximately 400,000 customers in Iceland to more than 400 million customers in the Continental European synchronous area.

Recurrent frequency deviations occur across a broad range of time scales. Figure 2 summarizes the dominant Fourier peaks and characteristic patterns observed in the Continental European grid.

At slow time scales, pronounced peaks occur at periods of 15 min and 60 min together with their higher harmonics. These deterministic frequency deviations (DFDs) arise from electricity trading, where generation is adjusted approximately stepwise at the boundaries of market intervals while electrical demand evolves continuously25,26,27,28. This phenomenon is not unique to the Continental European grid; it also appears in other markets such as Scandinavia (cf. Supplementary Fig. 1).

Additional recurrent patterns occur at shorter time scales, including oscillations due to load variations in data centres29, inter-area oscillations between geographically separated groups of synchronous generators30 and persistent forced oscillations of previously unresolved origin31 with periods of one to a few seconds. At even faster time scales, recent studies have identified sustained oscillations related to interactions of grid dynamics and data centres32 as well as subsynchronous oscillations in inverter-dominated grids33.

Against this background, we observe a remarkably narrow spectral peak at exactly 1 min in all investigated grids (Fig. 1). The signal is particularly pronounced in large interconnected systems in Europe, North America and Australia, but remains clearly visible in smaller grids such as Malaysia, South Korea and South Africa, as well as in island systems such as Mallorca, Ireland and Iceland. Its extremely narrow spectrum indicates a precisely timed and phase-coherent process that remains stable over multiple years. Related oscillations with characteristic periods around 1 min have previously been described as ULFOs13. In addition, we observe a weaker peak around 100 s whose origin remains uncertain. What causes this universal 1-min pattern?

Characterization of the 1-min pattern

To characterize the universal 1-min pattern, we divide the frequency time series f(t) into chunks of length τ. For every chunk, we compute a characteristic minutely profile s(t), referred to as the waveform in the following. More precisely, s(t) is the average over all minutes in the respective chunk for a given second of the minute t (see the Methods for details). Typically, we use chunks of length τ = 1 h to remove the hourly patterns induced by market effects, indicated by the subscript h. Furthermore, we define the hourly amplitude as

$${a}_{{h}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathop{\max }\limits_{t}({s}_{{h}}(t))-\mathop{\min }\limits_{t}({s}_{{h}}(t))\right)$$

(1)

to characterize the strength of the pattern.

Figure 3a–c summarizes the waveform properties for the Continental European grid. The amplitude exhibits a pronounced daily modulation, with weaker signals during the night and stronger amplitudes during daytime operation. By contrast, the waveform shape remains remarkably stable: the frequency is typically highest at the beginning of the minute and then decreases for a few seconds (night) up to half a minute (day). Comparable behaviour is observed across multiple grids (Supplementary Fig. 2), although the precise waveform shape differs between systems.

The waveform remains phase-coherent and stable over multiple years across all investigated grids. Such long-term coherence is difficult to reconcile with intrinsic oscillatory modes of the power system and instead points to drivers synchronized to a stable external clock.

These observations indicate a recurrent pattern in the underlying power imbalance. In general, the rate of change of frequency follows the instantaneous power imbalance in the grid. The relation between both quantities can be described by the aggregated swing equation34,35,

$$\frac{2{E}_{\mathrm{rot}}}{{f}_{\mathrm{ref}}}\frac{{\rm{d}}f}{{\rm{d}}t}={\Delta }P(t)-D[\,f(t)-{f}_{\mathrm{ref}}],$$

(2)

where ΔP(t) denotes the power imbalance, D is a damping coefficient, fref the reference frequency and Erot denotes the total rotational energy of online synchronous machines, which quantifies the system inertia. Accordingly, the observed decrease in frequency implies that demand exceeds generation during the first half of each minute.

Load–frequency control can amplify disturbances at specific frequencies via feedback effects in the power imbalance ΔP(t). Hydropower turbine-governor systems have therefore been suggested as a possible origin of oscillations with periods around 1 min (refs. 15,16). Recent work has furthermore linked 1-min oscillations to periodic set-point updates in secondary frequency control36. We likewise observe a 1-min pattern in the secondary control set-point in German control areas (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Note 1).

To assess the role of hydropower dynamics, we perform complementary mechanistic and data-driven analyses (Supplementary Notes 2 and 3). A mechanistic model of hydropower plant control in the Scandinavian grid reveals an internal resonance that can produce oscillatory responses with characteristic periods around 1 min (Supplementary Figs. 4–9). In parallel, an explainable machine learning analysis identifies systematic correlations between hydropower commitment and the signal amplitude in the Continental European grid (Supplementary Fig. 10). However, neither analysis supports the interpretation that hydropower dynamics alone can explain the long-term phase coherence and cross-system reproducibility of the observed signal. Instead, the results indicate that hydropower can amplify externally driven coherent disturbances or generate transient incoherent oscillations with periods close to 1 min.

The characteristic shape of the 1-min pattern is remarkably stable, while its amplitude changes over time. Figure 3d shows the long-term evolution for three European grids where sufficient historical data are available. We observe that the amplitude increased substantially in the British and Scandinavian grids after 2015, reaching peak values in 2020.

One important factor contributing to the observed increase is the reduction of effective system inertia during the transition from synchronous generation to converter-interfaced renewable power sources17. According to equation (2), lower inertia increases the frequency response to a given coherent power imbalance. A temporary increase during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) lockdown in 2020 further suggests that changes in system conditions, including effective system inertia and demand composition, can enhance the signal amplitude (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Note 4).

In the Scandinavian grid, amplitudes regularly exceeded 10 mHz, raising concerns among grid operators and utilities because oscillatory operation contributes to wear and tear of hydropower turbines providing primary control18. As a consequence, Scandinavian transmission system operators revised the requirements for frequency containment reserves in 202319. The revised prequalification procedures include sine-wave tests in the range of 10–300 s together with stricter damping and activation requirements intended to suppress oscillatory responses (cf. Supplementary Note 5). Following these revisions, amplitudes above 10 mHz were no longer observed in 2025 (Fig. 3d). These developments demonstrate that the recurrent 1-min oscillation already has operational relevance, even though current mitigation strategies primarily address amplification rather than the underlying source.

Identifying the drivers of the 1-min pattern

Frequency deviations reflect imbalances between generation and load as described by equation (2). For the Continental European synchronous area, the overall network power-frequency characteristic is estimated as K = 26.68 GW Hz−1 (ref. 20). Accordingly, a periodic frequency variation with an amplitude of 3 mHz corresponds to a coherent power variation of approximately 80 MW. Which devices generate this signal?

A comparison of measurements from the transmission and distribution levels in the Continental European grid shows that the waveform at the distribution level is slightly phase-advanced (Supplementary Figs. 12–14 and Supplementary Note 6). This suggests that the driving sources are located in the distribution system, while large synchronous generators respond to the resulting imbalance. We therefore investigate high-resolution measurements from local distribution networks.

Forschungszentrum Jülich operates a comprehensive measurement infrastructure in its campus distribution grid. We analyse high-resolution active-power measurements from multiple buildings and transformers to identify potential drivers of the signal (cf. Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary Note 7).

A clear 1-min component is observed only at a subset of measurement locations. In total, four buildings exhibit a pronounced periodic signal in the bus-level active-power measurements (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs. 16–17). Importantly, all affected buildings contain battery-based UPSs, whereas none of the measured buildings without UPS installations show such a pattern.

These observations identify battery energy management systems (EMSs), particularly in UPS applications, as prime drivers of the 1-min signal. Their control algorithms can induce periodic power-demand peaks at fixed 1-min intervals that propagate through the grid and contribute to coherent frequency variations. More generally, the results point to a broader class of cyber–physical synchronization effects arising from digitally controlled devices operating on externally synchronized clocks or periodic software routines.

To investigate this mechanism in detail, we analyse a UPS installation in Building 3 on the Jülich campus (Supplementary Table 1). Measurements include (1) the DC battery current, (2) AC currents and power flows upstream and downstream of the UPS and (3) transformer-level power flows up to the connection with the public 110-kV high-voltage grid (Fig. 5a).

On the DC side of the UPS, at the battery terminals, narrow current peaks recurring every 1 min are observed (Fig. 5b). The origin of these current peaks was independently confirmed by the manufacturer to be periodic self-tests executed by the battery EMS, confirming our interpretation. Similar minute-scale diagnostic routines are also documented for other commercial UPS systems (cf. Supplementary Note 8). On the AC side, after subtracting the demand of the building connected downstream of the UPS, these 1-min peaks are not clearly visible in the three-phase current time series. However, Fourier analysis reveals a persistent 1-min component in the currents (Fig. 5c). The corresponding periodic power demand can be traced across several transformers and a bus bar, up to the transformer connecting to the public 110-kV grid (Fig. 5d). These findings demonstrate that the pattern originating on the DC side propagates into the high-voltage grid.

We next investigate the temporal stability of the signal and the role of clock synchronization. Figure 6a compares weekly waveforms sw(t) for three transformers supplying buildings equipped with UPS systems. The measurements reveal markedly different behaviours.

In Building 3, the waveform exhibits a slowly drifting sawtooth-like structure characterized by a sharp increase followed by a gradual decay. This behaviour is consistent with the response of the UPS and grid infrastructure to narrow periodic current peaks on the DC side, effectively acting as a low-pass filter.

By contrast, Buildings 1 and 4 show phase-stable waveforms with recurring power-demand peaks shortly after the beginning of each minute. Such coherent demand peaks naturally explain the emergence of the corresponding frequency pattern at grid level. Notably, the UPS systems in Buildings 1 and 4 originate from different manufacturers than the system installed in Building 3, emphasizing the importance of EMS implementation and control design.

Synchronization of digitally controlled devices is commonly achieved using the Network Time Protocol (NTP)37, which synchronizes local clocks to Coordinated Universal Time (UTC). To assess the influence of clock synchronization on UPS operation, we performed comparative measurements for the Building 3 EMS with NTP synchronization disabled and enabled.

With NTP disabled, the UPS self-test signatures show a slow drift with respect to UTC. With NTP enabled, the daily progression of the UPS self-test signatures becomes substantially more pronounced, reaching approximately 6 s per day (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. 18 and Supplementary Note 9). The progression is not continuous; instead, the phase remains stable during the day and is shifted at midnight. These observations indicate that periodic EMS routines operate on internally scheduled 1-min cycles whose timing is regularly reinitialized through external clock synchronization.

Future developments and mitigation

The observed 1-min signal is already substantial in several power systems, although it has not reached a safety critical level. Nevertheless, several ongoing developments in modern energy systems make a further increase plausible and suggest that mitigation strategies should be implemented before the effect becomes more severe.

One important trend is the rapid deployment of digitally controlled energy devices. Battery capacities are increasing strongly38,39 (Fig. 7a), while data centres equipped with large UPS installations account for a growing fraction of electricity demand7,40. In Ireland, where data centres reached approximately 18% of national electricity demand in 202241, the magnitude of the minute-scale frequency drop increased from approximately 3 mHz to 12 mHz between 2016 and 2021 (Fig. 7b). More recent measurements show that individual frequency drops can exceed 20 mHz and, when superimposed on larger frequency excursions, can bring the system close to the limits of the target operating range (Supplementary Figs. 19–21 and Supplementary Note 10).

In parallel, the proliferation of IoT devices42 introduces additional loads operating with externally synchronized clocks or periodic software routines. We observe corresponding periodic power signatures both in a smart baking oven and in Raspberry-Pi clusters (Fig. 7c, Supplementary Figs. 22 and 23 and Supplementary Note 11).

A second important trend is the reduction of system inertia during the transition from fossil-fuelled synchronous generation to converter-interfaced renewable generation. Lower inertia increases the frequency response to a given coherent power imbalance17 and has been linked to stronger ULFOs14. This effect is illustrated by the Mallorca grid, where the withdrawal of a major coal-fired power plant from regular operation at the end of 2019 coincided with an increase of the 1-min amplitude by approximately 50% (Fig. 7d, Supplementary Fig. 24 and Supplementary Note 12). This increase is consistent with a reduction in effective inertia, although concurrent changes in frequency-control operation may also have contributed.

These examples indicate that the 1-min signal can grow through both sides of the frequency response: coherent power variations may increase as clocked devices proliferate, while the same power variations lead to larger frequency deviations as inertia decreases. This does not imply an imminent stability threat, but it makes the phenomenon relevant for future system operation, especially because deterministic deviations can add to stochastic fluctuations and other recurrent patterns (Supplementary Figs. 25–30 and Supplementary Note 13).

Several mitigation routes are available. System-level measures can reduce the frequency impact of deterministic disturbances. In particular, revisions of frequency-control regulations in the Scandinavian19 and Australian43 grids substantially reduced the amplitude of the observed signal (Fig. 7e). However, such measures primarily mitigate the frequency response rather than the underlying coherent power modulation.

The root cause of cyber–physical power oscillations can be addressed directly through control design. Clocked internal routines should avoid simultaneous power adjustments across many devices, for example, by using randomized scheduling, adaptive offsets or event-driven rather than strictly time-driven execution. This approach is technically feasible for utility-scale batteries and UPS systems, where software updates can be deployed centrally. Indeed, following our communication with the manufacturer, a software update was issued that removed the strict periodicity of the current peaks in one battery block of the investigated UPS system (Fig. 7f). However, self-tests still occur at discrete times such that the root cause has been resolved only partially (Supplementary Fig. 31 and Supplementary Note 9).

Mitigation may be more challenging for household-scale IoT devices, where software updates and control standards are less coordinated than in utility-scale infrastructure. These observations highlight the importance of early coordination between manufacturers, system operators and regulators as digitally synchronized energy devices continue to proliferate.

Discussion

Cybersecurity and the management of power imbalances are widely recognized as central challenges for future power systems44,45. Maintaining a stable frequency becomes increasingly difficult as system inertia declines17,22, while cybersecurity is threatened by failures and malicious attacks9. Here, we highlight a more subtle systemic challenge arising from the cyber–physical coupling of modern power systems.

We show that the grid frequency in power systems across five continents exhibits a persistent and highly stable recurrence with a period of exactly 1 min. While aspects of such ULFOs have been reported previously36, the origin of their long-term coherence has remained unclear. We provide evidence that the observed signal is probably linked to unintended synchronization of digitally controlled devices whose control actions are scheduled by clocked software processes. When synchronized across many devices, such routines can generate coherent power variations at system scale. The growing relevance of this mechanism is illustrated by the Irish power system, where the initial frequency drop of the 1-min signal increased to 12 mHz in 2021, reaching substantially larger values during individual periods (Supplementary Fig. 20). This increase coincides with the rapid expansion of data centres equipped with large UPS installations, whose electricity demand reached approximately 18% of national consumption in 2022. The growing importance of data centres for power-system planning and operation has brought renewed attention to their dynamic interaction with the grid7,46, but the role of synchronized UPS control has largely been overlooked. Future work should develop detailed mechanistic models that capture the interaction between power-system operation and digitally controlled loads, including data centres and clock-synchronized devices.

More generally, many modern energy devices, including large-scale batteries, photovoltaic inverters and smart appliances, rely on embedded software and external time synchronization and may therefore contribute to coherent cyber–physical power oscillations. Beyond the highly coherent behaviour observed here, model-based studies suggest that even partial temporal coherence across large device populations may already produce measurable aggregate effects on the power balance47. The rapid projected expansion of battery storage systems and IoT devices is therefore likely to increase the relevance of such synchronization effects in future power systems. At the same time, decreasing system inertia increases the frequency response to coherent power imbalances17, while hydropower turbine-governor dynamics may further amplify coherent oscillations through resonant feedback effects.

Mitigation can proceed along two complementary routes. System-level measures, such as stricter primary frequency-control requirements, can reduce the frequency impact of deterministic disturbances, as observed in the Scandinavian and Australian grids. However, such measures mainly suppress the response to coherent power modulation rather than its origin. Addressing the root cause requires improved control design, for example through randomized scheduling, adaptive offsets or event-driven instead of strictly time-driven routines. Our measurements demonstrate that such mitigation is technically feasible, although coordinated implementation across heterogeneous large-scale device populations remains challenging.

Our results highlight the increasingly important role of software and digital control in modern energy systems. Traditionally, power-system stability has primarily been analysed in terms of physical infrastructure and control dynamics. Increasing digitalization, however, requires an integrated perspective in which control logic, software timing and synchronization are treated as integral components of power-system stability. More broadly, our findings demonstrate that collective effects are central to future cyber–physical power systems and can emerge both through interactions in the physical grid and through digital interconnections.

Methods

Data sources and processing

To monitor grid behaviour, load profiles and power flows on the Jülich research campus, over 80 PQI-DA smart power quality (PQ) meters by a-eberle have been deployed48. These devices comply with the following PQ standards: EN 50160, IEEE 519, IEC 61000-2-2, IEC 61000-4-30, IEC 61000-2-12, IEC 61000-4-7, IEC 61000-2-4 and IEC 61000-4-15, as well as NRS 048. In particular, they are Class A devices that comply with the IEC 61000-4-30 Ed. 3 (2015) standards49, ensuring highly accurate measurements of frequency, voltage and current at the fundamental frequency, as well as other PQ phenomena. Communication with the meters relies on the Modbus Transmission Control Protocol, which provides data via standardized registers for various aggregation intervals, from 200 ms up to 2 h, as defined in the IEC standards50,51. During normal operation, key electrical quantities such as frequency, voltage, current and power are stored on the FIWARE-based Campus Information and Communications Technology platform52. We use data with 3-s resolution. Additional details are provided in Supplementary Note 7.

Frequency data with a resolution of 1 s on transmission grid-level for the Continental European, British and Scandinavian grids are provided by the transmission system operators53,54,55. The data have been pre-processed using the scripts provided in ref. 56. We use data from 1 January 2015 until 31 December 2025. For Australia, frequency data is available with 4-s resolution from the transmission system operator57. In addition, we obtained frequency data from Ireland for 2016 with 1-s resolution directly from the network operator EirGrid. Low-resolution frequency data in Ireland for 1 month in 2026 has been scraped directly from EirGrid’s Smart Grid Dashboard58.

Frequency data for USA, Malaysia, South Korea, South Africa, Mallorca, Ireland (2021) and Iceland are measured on the distribution grid-level using the Electrical Data Recorder described in refs. 59,60; details are provided in Supplementary Note 14. The Electrical Data Recorder measures voltage and current waveforms at high temporal resolution and derives the fundamental system frequency using a filtered zero-crossing method61. This approach is well suited for capturing frequency variations on time scales of seconds and above, as considered in this study, whereas the analysis of faster dynamics or strongly modulated signals may require alternative frequency definitions and demodulation methods62. These standalone measurements vary in length and cover approximately 5 years (Mallorca), 10 months (US West), 4 months (South Africa), 2.5 weeks (US East), 2.5 months (Iceland), 3.5 months (South Korea), 1 month (Ireland, 2021) and 1.5 weeks (Malaysia). A comparison of frequency data from the three different sources is provided in Supplementary Note 14 and Supplementary Fig. 32. The exact measurement periods are provided in Supplementary Figs. 33–35.

Frequency data from Belfort, Málaga and Porto for illustrating the inter-area oscillations over a period of 20 s, with a time resolution of 0.1 s in Fig. 2d, is provided by the Gridradar initiative63. The remaining data from Karlsruhe, Elk and Kaunas in Fig. 2d are also measured by Electrical Data Recorder devices.

The data on electricity generation from the coal-fired power plant in Mallorca are taken from ref. 64. In our analysis, the 5-min resolution dataset is aggregated into daily averages.

If not explicitly stated otherwise, we use UTC to index the time series in order to avoid ambiguity due to daylight saving time. As we analyse the hourly amplitudes, we further remove full hours of time series data if a single data point is missing or corrupted. The missing data after pre-processing is summarized in Supplementary Figs. 33–35.

Waveform and amplitude

Let f(t) be a discrete time series for some time interval t ∈ [t0, t1] of length τ = t1 − t0 with sampling time Δt. In particular, we consider the grid frequency and the real power load. We compute the average waveform s(t) of a recurrent temporal pattern with period T by averaging over blocks of length T. That is, we divide the interval [t0, t1] into \(N=\frac{{t}_{1}-{t}_{0}}{T}\) blocks of length T and average f(t) over these blocks.

$${s}_{[{t}_{0},{t}_{1}],T}(t):=\frac{1}{N}\sum _{n=0}^{N-1}f({t}_{0}+nT+t)-{\langle f(t)\rangle }_{[{t}_{0},{t}_{1}]},$$

(3)

for t ∈ [0, T). From the waveform, we define the amplitude \({a}_{[{t}_{0},{t}_{1}],T}\) of the pattern over the interval [t0, t1] as

$${a}_{[{t}_{0},{t}_{1}],T}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathop{\max }\limits_{t\in [0,T)}{s}_{[{t}_{0},{t}_{1}],T}(t)-\mathop{\min }\limits_{t\in [0,T)}{s}_{[{t}_{0},{t}_{1}],T}(t)\right).$$

(4)

To quantify how waveforms and amplitudes evolve over time, we divide the entire measurement period into non-overlapping 1-h segments and evaluate the waveform and its amplitude separately for each segment. Specifically, for each hour h we set t0 = h and t1 = h + 1 h, and denote the resulting waveform as sh,T(t) and the corresponding amplitude as ah,T. For simplicity, we drop the subscript \(T=1\,\min\) in the main text.

Similarly, we define daily sd,T(t), weekly sw,T(t) and monthly sm,T(t) waveforms and amplitudes by setting τ to 1 day, 1 week and 1 month, respectively, and averaging over the corresponding number of blocks N.
