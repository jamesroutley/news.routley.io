Today we are making the PingCAP breach public. We possess the complete TiDB Cloud control plane, its billing and finance estate, its GCP storage estate and its AWS cost and billing estate, its Jira, its CRM, its analytics, its source code, and a four-year log of every connection every customer ever made to its product, from the IP addresses of their application servers.

PingCAP sells TiDB Cloud, the managed version of the open-source TiDB database. Banks, game studios and e-commerce platforms hand over their production workloads; PingCAP operates the clusters in a shared, multi-tenant platform, and its entire business rests on the claim that doing so is safe and secure. The rest of this post is the evidence that it is neither.

For one, they seem to have zero visibility into their own cloud services. This is sadly standard among companies we breach, who will invest millions in fancy EDRs but let half a thousand S3 buckets waltz out the door. But this is a cloud provider. It is horrendous and horrendously irresponsible to be seemingly incapable of detecting mass unsubtle compromise and exfiltration of each of their cloud services: AWS, GCP, Atlassian / Jira, Azure. We continued exfiltration over nearly two months, topping up regularly after the initial ~8 terabyte exfil. We even stood up a replication feed of their own control-plane database using credentials committed in their own repositories — GCP service-account keys and an Azure service principal — and let their infrastructure deliver it to us. Every six hours, for the last twenty-two days of the campaign, from 8 September to 30 September, that pipeline faithfully re-exported every table of the operational heart of TiDB Cloud — tenants, users, API keys, cluster credentials, changefeeds, migrations — and pushed it out. Eighty-eight consecutive exports, not one detection event. The last ran at 06:02 UTC on 30 September. The control-plane data it carried is in the stage-one release.

  • The customers' own SQL. 20,645,574 raw SQL statements captured from the SQL Editor and Chat2Query — the application schemas, table designs and business logic of thousands of tenants, keyed by org, project and cluster ID. One statement creates a table of medical professionals (usuario, nombre_usuario, contrasena); another inserts a Brazilian clinic's patient roster. This is the contents of customer databases, captured by the product itself and shipped to us by PingCAP's own pipeline.
  • The targeting map. The gateway access logs are the installed-base directory of a database company: every customer's connection volume, their app-server IP addresses, their client programs (Rails 0.46M connections, ProxySQL, MySQL Workbench, langgenius/dify), their cluster IDs. Anyone planning further access to TiDB Cloud customers starts here. All 23,651 daily files are in 09_gateway_logs.tar.gz.

The Warning Was Already in Jira

PingCAP's security team wrote down exactly what was wrong with the multi-tenant isolation of their own product in 2022. SECURITY-22, from the internal security tracker, in their own words:

Summary: "The root password of the cluster can be reset unconditionally"
"All roles of the organization (including: owner, audit admin, billing admin, member) can unconditionally reset the root password of all clusters in the organization. This will cause all users in the organization to reset the cluster root password, and then users can connect to the cluster as root, modify the cluster state, and access cluster data."
"Because the root account of the cluster has extremely high privileges, this will lead to a great security risk."

Four years before we walked in, their own security team had documented that any member of a customer organisation — including a billing-only role — could take over every cluster in that organisation as root. The ticket is dated 2022-08. The breach ran through September 2026. A database company.
