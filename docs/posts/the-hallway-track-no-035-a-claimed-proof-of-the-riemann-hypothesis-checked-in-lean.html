The Hallway Track No. 035: A claimed proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, checked in Lean

On November 20, Levent Alpöge released a claimed proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, made with Claude, as about 17 million lines of Lean code and no human-readable paper. The next day the Lean and Mathlib communities certified that the code proves Mathlib's statement of the Riemann Hypothesis. The checks they ran correspond to four questions that Kevin Buzzard had posted eight days earlier: whether the formal statement matches the informal one, whether the code compiles, whether it proves the stated theorem, and whether it relies on a bug in Lean's kernel. These checks establish that the code proves the statement, but they do not explain the proof. Terry Tao places the result at the far end of a range from human-readable proofs to proofs that exist only in Lean. On the same day, Ravi Vakil posted a 940-page human-written draft proof of the same hypothesis by Alessandro Masullo, and within hours readers using AI models reported problems in it.

The Claim and the First Reactions

  • Levent Alpöge's statement on a Claude-assisted proof of the Riemann Hypothesis

    Levent Alpöge, November 20 2026

    Alpöge, a mathematician employed by Anthropic, released his claimed proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis as Lean code only, explaining that written versions ran to more than 250,000 pages; he noted that the announcement timing was "subject to a constraint to respect" that he discussed with Anthropic's leadership.

  • A claimed proof of the Riemann hypothesis

    Quanta Magazine (Konstantin Kakaes), November 20 2026

    Kakaes contrasts Alpöge's months of repeated, expert-guided work with Claude against OpenAI's October release of 377 results, most from a single prompt, and quotes number theorist Filippo Nuccio on the argument drawing on most of the number theory of the last 50 years; if the Lean code is verified, Kakaes writes, mathematicians will probably accept the proof before anyone understands it.

  • Anthropic mathematician proves Riemann hypothesis, most famous problem in all of math

    Scientific American (Joseph Howlett), November 20 2026, updated November 21

    Andrew Sutherland (MIT), who compiled the code independently, calls the result "an historic moment," and Hector Pasten (Pontifical Catholic University of Chile) and Carlo Pagano (Concordia University) discuss what the new ideas could open up for number theory if the proof holds.

  • The stochastic parrots have apparently proved the Riemann Hypothesis

    Scott Aaronson, November 20 2026

    Aaronson expects the proof to stand because a Lean certificate is the strongest check available, while noting that "mathematicians of course won't be satisfied with 17 million lines of Lean" and that distilling the ideas will take years; a November 21 update reports the Lean community's independent verification.

Checking a Proof Written in Lean

  • ...and the proof is checked in Lean

    Kevin Buzzard (Imperial College London), November 12 2026

    Written before the Riemann claim, the post sets out four questions for any AI-generated Lean repository (does the statement match the informal statement, does the code compile, does the proof prove the theorem as stated, does it exploit a kernel bug), names lake comparator and its --paranoid mode as the tools for the last two, and notes that these tests have not yet been run on many of OpenAI's October results.

  • As mathematicians race to verify AI Riemann proof, a human proof emerges

    Quanta Magazine (Konstantin Kakaes), November 20 2026

    Kakaes describes the checks in the order they were run: Sutherland and then Simon DeDeo (CMU) compiled the code, it was compiled again with independent versions of Lean to rule out a Lean bug, and a comparator program checked that no key definitions had been changed to make the statement trivially true, a step still running at the time of the post.

  • Lean verification of the Riemann Hypothesis

    Lean and Mathlib communities, November 21 2026

    The joint statement, endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, reports that two independent tools (comparator and con-leche --compare) confirmed that the proved theorem matches Mathlib's existing RiemannHypothesis statement, and that Lean's kernel and several independently written checkers accepted the exported proof; the statement certifies formal correctness and leaves the new mathematical ideas for future work to explain.

Proofs That People Can and Cannot Read

  • Unforced Euler singularity, unforced Navier-Stokes singularity, and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis

    Terry Tao, November 20 2026

    Tao orders three AI-assisted results announced the same day by how "digested" they are: a human-readable claimed proof of Euler blowup, a readable but incomplete claimed proof of Navier-Stokes blowup, and a Lean-only claimed proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis that, on a first look, draws on much of the Langlands program and arithmetic geometry.

  • Hurried Announcement: Alessandro Masullo GRH

    Ravi Vakil (Stanford), November 20 2026

    Vakil posted Masullo's 940-page draft proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, written without AI, a year earlier than planned (Brian Conrad had been reading it for months); about eight hours later Vakil added that "several people have contacted me to report problems in the proposed proof, identified with the assistance of different AI models."

  • Navier-Stokes lost in translation: Why Lean verification of AI autoformalisation does not guarantee correct natural language proofs

    arXiv (Bastounis, Circelli, Hansen), October 6 2026

    The authors argue that when an AI translates a written proof into Lean, a successful Lean check may say nothing about the written argument, and they show that the Lean proof accompanying OpenAI's announced Navier-Stokes blowup result does not correspond to the written proof; the Riemann claim avoids this problem by having no written proof, which leaves the Lean statement as the only thing to check.
