How do you take a picture of a black hole?

Black holes are one of the universe’s most exotic and elusive objects. When nothing, not even light, can escape their powerful gravitational grasp, taking a picture of one seems unimaginable. But an image of a black hole, or more accurately its shadow, could offer insights that theory alone may not provide when testing predictions. So how can it be done?

Harvard astrophysicist Shep Doeleman and a team of 250 researchers achieved this extraordinary feat by linking a group of radio telescopes around the world. This network, called the Event Horizon Telescope, produced the first image of a supermassive black hole in the galaxy M87, around 55 million light-years away, and the first image of the black hole at the heart of our galaxy. In this episode, Doeleman tells co-host Janna Levin how he turned the Earth into a telescope capable of seeing a black hole, the incredible level of science and collaboration that was required to achieve this, how the images have furthered our understanding of these exotic objects, and how movies of black holes may be possible by the early 2030s.

DOELEMAN: It’s a slow burn in a certain way. So, the first thing you see is just a graph, a two-dimensional graph. And you see on the Y-axis, it’s the amount of energy the two telescopes that are widely separated are seeing. On the X-axis, it’s the distance between the telescopes. And what happens is that if you see a ring, you’d see a characteristic bounce in this graph, and that’s what we saw. I think there’s a picture of me at a fancy dinner. So that was the first indication that we had something. And then we started imaging. So we had been perfecting these imaging algorithms on synthetic data. We’d fed these algorithms made-up data. So we would take a black hole image that we might be looking at. We’d corrupt it with noise, and we’d send it through these algorithms, and we’d refine these to the point where we were pretty confident in these algorithms.

But before we set these loose on the data, we also realized that human error or human groupthink could be a really big problem. So we split our teams up into four groups. Each group had the data, but they could not talk to each other because we wanted to make sure that we all weren’t in the same room thinking like, “I see a ring. Do you see a ring? I see a ring.” And it’s not a ring, right?

So we had four different geographically separated groups, and they all used different algorithms. And then we all came together to the building where I am sitting right now, the Black Hole Initiative at Harvard in July of 2018, and we were in the basement of this building, and the four teams put up their images, and they were all rings.

That was a moment where there was a lot of crying and screaming. Uh, and, and then we all, and then we spent the next six months trying to make those rings go away. So we would train the algorithms on everything except for rings.

So they became very good at imaging double sources, or they became very good at imaging filled disks. And then we would set them loose on the data, and they would always come back to the ring. And that’s when we also knew in a more robust way that the ring image was real.

DOELEMAN: Yeah. The way this works is that when you’re so close to the black hole, the paths of light, which normally would travel in a straight line, is forced to travel along the curved spacetime. And so it’s really tracing the metric of spacetime. It’s tracing the gravitational well that it finds itself in.

So when light leaves hot gas that’s swirling around the galactic center, let’s say, it starts off, but then instead of going in some straight line, it’ll curve around and come to you. So all the light from behind the black hole is being lensed around to form this ring.

And it’s the size of this ring that is given by Einstein’s equations. And you get these crazy factors like two the square root of 27. G is Newton’s gravitational constant, M is the mass of the black hole, c is the speed of light. But you realize that the diameter of this ring basically just controlled by the mass of the black hole.

DOELEMAN: Yeah, yeah. Now the cool thing is that imagine there was an astronomer on the other side of the galaxy. They would see a ring too, because they would see all the light from where we are lensed around in their direction. So if anyone’s curious about testing Einstein’s theories or whatever theories have been concocted by another civilization, they would be curious about this same object that we’re looking at.

DOELEMAN: I know. It’s in the most unimaginative, unoriginal way possible. So we’ve taken still images of black holes, and with any great discovery, they just raise the next set of questions. So the next natural thing to ask is, can we make a movie of the black hole and see not just light bending around, but the motion of matter as it orbits the black hole?

So there’s every reason for us to keep going, and the key is adding more radio dishes around the globe.

If we can add more radio dishes, we’ll fill in the Earth-sized virtual lens much faster. The number of connections goes as N times N minus one, so there’s a quadratic factor in there. So you don’t just get one more telescope, you get all the connections to that telescope. And also, having more telescopes ensures that as the Earth turns, you always have a critical number to make the next frame in a movie.

Five sites that are critical to allowing this to happen: One is in Chile, one is in Mexico, one is in Wyoming, and one is in the Canary Islands, and a fifth in Tanzania on the slopes of Mount Kilimanjaro would also be very, very nice to have. If we had all those, and we were observing at the highest frequencies, we can absolutely make movies of black holes, and high definition, high sensitivity, full color movies of black holes.

And this is the next step. I’ll just say we’ve secured all the funding to build the first telescope in the Canary Islands. I’ve now summited Mount Kilimanjaro twice, scouting for locations to build a telescope up there.

DOELEMAN: This is what I live for. LEVIN: And what’s the payoff for a movie scientifically? I mean, obviously it’s gonna be thrilling. I’m gonna wanna see it. But beyond just the thrill of it, what can I extract from watching a movie that I can’t from a still image?

DOELEMAN: So there’s a couple things. So one is that spin is elusive. So, a, a black hole basically has only a few characteristics that define it. Its mass, its spin, maybe its charge, but most of the astrophysical black holes that we know of probably have charge equalized, because there’s plasma around it that would do that. So the mass is pretty easy. The size of this ring is directly related to mass. We’ve checked that one off.

But the spin of the black hole imprints its history, like how it grew over time, and the spin also tells you about the frame dragging, the warping of spacetime as it spins. And to get a handle on that, you need to really look at dynamics of matter.

To give you a sense of the problem, if you have a black hole and it’s spinning not at all, you get one size of the ring related to its mass. If it’s spinning maximally, the fastest it can ever spin, you see the same ring, but it’s just about 4% smaller, okay? So spin is not measured well by looking at the shadow of the black hole that we’ve already seen.

But the time it takes matter to orbit the black hole, that for Sagittarius A* ranges from half an hour if it’s not spinning to four minutes if it’s maximally spinning. Now we have an indicator, now we have a measurement we can make that would tell us cleanly what the spin is. So, the biggest payoff for astronomers and physicists is that we can make a precise measurement of spin by looking at matter orbiting the black hole.
