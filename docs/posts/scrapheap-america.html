Scrapheap America

Pretty sure everyone should be wearing gloves here.

I was in bed with the flu as America fell apart. I drifted in and out of fever dreams and incessant coughing jags, emerging long enough to discover yet another piece of critical infrastructure had suddenly failed. First it was email, then it was airplanes, then banks, then I lost track somewhere in the haze as one day blurred into two and then into three. Emerging from the other side, my lungs wrecked, my head aching—damaged, like the song says, bad at best—I felt like maybe I'd fared better than the various systems we rely on to, you know, function.

This isn't a post-mortem on what happened when everything went down. That's been done better than I could by the folks at 404 Media, who summed it up as the "philosophy of firing the experts and YOLOing AI into everything while hoping shit doesn’t break is now the explicit strategy of our entire economy and our entire government." It's not working.

It isn't just computer systems, which have been hit by a one-two punch of years of mass layoffs followed by AI-exposed security vulnerabilities that could have really used those hundreds of thousands of extra hands on deck right about now. It's pretty much every overwhelmed governmental entity, limping along since DOGE blew through last year like a tornado. It's your bank account, which if it's like mine, has entirely given up. It's gas prices. It's plane tickets. It's the cost of bread and shoes and every other goddamn thing.

As my pal Bree Bridges put it in the midst of the waterfall of breakdowns last week: "Everything costs too much and nothing works. You go broke paying for insurance that you can't even access until you pay even more money and after that they will try to prevent you from getting benefits in any way they can. food is poison, measles are back, cheeseburgers are luxury."

Things have been breaking for a long time now. The thing about having our country run by incompetents, grifters, and sycophants is that "broken" was always going to be the end state, it was just a question of how long it might take. Well, now we know: it's now. The whole country is peeling up like the Reflecting Pool liner.

When I'm as sick as I was in the last week (seriously, get your flu shot), there's one show that my body can handle: The late 1990s British engineering competition Scrapheap Challenge. I'm not sure why my body, when it's struggling to survive, wants to watch teams of working-class Brits run through a junkyard using a plasma cutter to separate an axle from an old London taxi, pull the motor from an old washing machine, or salvage a junked airplane and then make it fly, but Scrapheap has accompanied pretty much every major illness I've had for the last quarter century.

The premise of Scrapheap Challenge is simple. Two teams of three compete to build, over 10 hours, fully working machines capable of meeting whatever challenge has been laid out. That's meant teams building working trebuchets large enough to fling a car, firetrucks that can put out a blaze, demolition machines that can tear down walls, and every possible vehicle you can imagine and some you can not. All of it built from junked cars and rusted beams and every piece of scrap that's ever been tossed out. It's wildly inventive, often quite funny, and 100% a show that could not be made today because it's amazing that nobody ever cut off a body part or set themselves on fire.

At this point, I've seen every Challenge that's been lovingly preserved on YouTube, and so this past week I've drifted in and out of consciousness, half watching, half listening, and half discovering that, sandwiched between the teams "bodging" some contraption together, another system had collapsed in America.

The thing about everything breaking is that we have no real choice but to fix it. That's the challenge we all face right now in America, as things fall apart and there's nobody left to put it back together but us. But there's nothing saying that it has to resemble what came before. In Scrapheap Challenge parts are parts: raw materials to be reassembled into something that might work, given enough duct tape and crossed fingers. That's everything now: a vast field of scrap ready to be rebuilt into whatever we might need. The opportunity for imagination, for envisioning a life that's better than the one we've seen collapse around us, for building new and better and different is upon us. America is a scrapheap now, and it's up to us to rise to the challenge.
