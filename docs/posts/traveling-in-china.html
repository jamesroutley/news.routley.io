Traveling in China

I spent the past four weeks in China: two weeks in Hong Kong for work, and two weeks traveling solo on the mainland. These are my notes on the logistics of traveling in China as a foreigner.

This information comes from my personal experience in November 2026. Visa policies, VPN availability, etc. may change in the future, and your individual situation may differ from mine.

Entry

Mainland China and Hong Kong have separate border controls. US passport holders can enter Hong Kong without prior permission, but must obtain a visa to enter mainland China. The visa application process begins with a lengthy online questionnaire at https://consular.mfa.gov.cn/VISA/. You can apply for a 10-year visa with multiple entries of up to 90 days each. You must submit a headshot photo that is different from the one in your passport. The photo requirements are exacting and the system will reject non-compliant photos, so it's worth having it taken by a professional studio that is familiar with China visa photos. Make sure that your ears and forehead are not covered by your hair: my first photo was rejected for this reason. You must pay a service fee as part of the application.

After completing the online questionnaire, you then make two trips to a China Visa Application Service Center to obtain the visa: once to drop off your passport, and again a week later to pick it up with the newly-issued visa pasted inside. The online questionnaire says you must bring a physical copy of your visa photo, but the consulate never asked me for mine.

Immigration at the Hong Kong–Shenzhen border crossing took me about 45 minutes. You first fill out an arrival card with basic information about your identity and travel plans, then you present your passport to an immigration officer. The officer took my photograph and fingerprints, but did not ask me any questions.

Payment

Three apps should suffice for payment almost everywhere in China: Alipay and WeChat for mainland China, and Octopus for Tourists for Hong Kong. In four weeks in China, I never used cash, not even with street vendors.

Make sure to set up Alipay and WeChat ahead of time. You may need to enter your passport details and collect biometrics. Alipay has a payment PIN code that you should memorize. Some vendors scan your QR code, while other vendors have you scan theirs. You may have to manually enter the purchase amount.

Both the Alipay and WeChat apps have English-language versions, though there is frequently untranslated Chinese text in dialogs and sub-menus. The first time I tried to use WeChat for payment, I struggled to find the scanner function. It's under the '+' button at the top-right of the main screen.

Octopus is Hong Kong's transit card, but it is also widely accepted by restaurants and other businesses. You can download the app or get a physical card. Alipay is also used in Hong Kong, but it is a different app from the mainland version which non-Hong Kong residents cannot activate. Use Octopus or your credit card instead.

Mainland China and Hong Kong use different currencies: the renminbi and the Hong Kong dollar. The Hong Kong dollar is often represented with a plain '$' symbol, so don't be alarmed when you see a bag of chips costs $15 – that's less than US$2 at time of writing.

Transport

Trains

China has a vast, modern high-speed rail network that connects all the major cities. It is convenient, inexpensive, and punctual. You can book tickets on Trip.com, which has a polished English-language user interface.

You must pass through security before entering train stations in China, so budget some extra time. Train stations in China are large and have comfortable waiting rooms with restaurants and convenience stores. Boarding usually starts 15 minutes before departure time. You do not receive a physical or digital ticket: to board the train, you present your passport. You often need to use one of staffed fare gates at the far left or right, as the standard gates do not always accept foreign passports.

China is a large country, and for some journeys, a flight may be preferable to a train ride, e.g., Hong Kong to Beijing takes less than 4 hours by air but more than 8 hours by rail.

Subways

Most large cities in China have a subway system. All but a few of them were constructed in the last twenty years, and the three that I experienced – the Hong Kong MTR, the Shenzhen Metro, and the Chengdu Metro – were modern, clean, and easy to navigate. In mainland China (but not in Hong Kong), you pass through security before entering like at the train station. This usually takes less than a minute. If you are carrying a water bottle or other liquid container, the officer will ask you to remove it from your bag so they can scan it with a handheld scanner, but you don't need to empty it like at the airport. You can pay by setting up a transit card inside of Alipay. Shenzhen and Chengdu also let you tap US credit cards.

Ride-hailing

DiDi is the Chinese version of Uber and Lyft, and it works the same way: you enter your destination, you are given a fare estimate, you are matched with a driver, and your journey is tracked by GPS on a map. You can call a DiDi from within the Alipay app. The driver usually expects you to confirm the last four digits of your phone number when you get into the car, so it's worth knowing how to say that in Chinese.

Navigation

I used Apple Maps to navigate in China. Amap is commonly used by locals, but I found the interface frustrating. Google Maps does not work properly in mainland China: locations are sometimes shifted hundreds of meters from their actual position.

Internet

VPNs

Some Western services, like Google, are not available in China. You can circumvent this by installing a VPN that tunnels your traffic outside of China. I used Astrill's OpenWeb VPN service, and it worked everywhere that I went in China, but the landscape is constantly changing, so you should do your own research. VPN use is illegal in China, so – although it is ubiquitous among foreign visitors and unlikely to get you in trouble – it may be prudent to avoid discussing it openly.

SIM cards

Just like international travel elsewhere, you may need a SIM card (or a digital eSIM) for your cell phone to get service in China. Mainland China, Hong Kong, and Macau may be considered different countries by the eSIM provider, so make sure the card you purchase works everywhere you plan to travel.

I purchased a data-only eSIM and kept my primary American eSIM so I could still receive SMS one-time codes and other messages. Receiving text messages did not incur roaming charges on my Verizon subscription, but your situation may be different.

Foreign eSIMs are already routed outside of China, so you don't need to use a VPN on your phone. I only installed the VPN on my computer.

Availability

Cellular internet was universally available wherever I traveled, not just in major cities but also in semi-rural areas. Public WiFi (e.g., in restaurants and shopping malls) in China usually requires SMS verification that only works with a Chinese phone number, but hotel WiFi worked fine.

Language

Spoken Chinese

Knowledge of spoken English in China is uneven. I met hotel clerks and waitstaff in rural Guangxi who were quite proficient in English, while many people in major cities knew little or none.

I've listed a few essential phrases below (in pinyin) that proved useful to me in practice. Chinese is a tonal language, and if you don't pronounce the tones with some semblance of accuracy, then native speakers will have trouble understanding you. How to pronounce Chinese is beyond the scope of this article, but there are many learning resources online; I like the Grace Mandarin Chinese YouTube channel.

  • Nǐ hǎo. – Hello. (This is used with strangers and the first time meeting a new person, but not between friends, who are likely to just say 'hello' or a more specific greeting like zǎoshang hǎo 'good morning'.)
  • Xièxie. – Thank you.
  • Hǎo de. – Okay. (Assenting to something.)
  • Bù yòng le. – No thank you. (To politely decline an offer.)
  • Bùkèqì. – You're welcome.
  • Bùhǎoyìsi. – I beg your pardon, or sorry. (The standard term for 'sorry' is duìbuqǐ, but that is for 'real' apologies rather than just being polite.)
  • Jièguò – Excuse me. (In the sense of 'make way' or 'let me through'.)
  • Méiguānxi. – It's OK. (In response to duìbuqǐ.)
  • Kěyǐ ma? – May I? (Asking permission to do something, most useful when pointing or miming the action.)
  • Duōshao qián? – How much does this cost? (The response will be a number followed by kuài, meaning renminbi.)
  • Wǒ yào zhège. – I'd like to have this one. (For pointing and ordering at a restaurant or store.)
  • Wǒ yào mǎidān. – I'd like to pay the bill.
  • Sǎo mǎ. – Scan the QR code. (This is a common way of ordering and paying in China. You may also hear wǒ sǎo nǐ 'I scan you' or nǐ sǎo wǒ 'You scan me'.)
  • Nǐmen yǒu méiyǒu zhège? – Do you have this thing? (E.g., pointing to something on your phone in a store.)
  • Háiyǒu ma? – Anything else?
  • Méiyǒu le. – Nothing else.

As mentioned in the ride-hailing section, it's worthwhile to know the last four digits of your phone number in Chinese. They are read digit-by-digit, so 1234 is yī èr sān sì. You should also know how to count up to the hundreds, or you won't be able to understand when people respond to your duōshao qián.

Written Chinese

If you learn these phrases, you should be able to conduct some simple interactions entirely in Chinese. You will not, however, be able to read Chinese, as real-life Chinese is not written in pinyin but in characters. There are thousands of characters in daily use, so learning to read Chinese is a daunting challenge. Fortunately, it is largely unnecessary for travelers, as almost all official signs are bilingual in Chinese and English. The most likely place you will encounter untranslated text is on restaurant menus, so if you do want to learn some Chinese characters, characters for foods and beverages would be most useful. Restaurant menus commonly have pictures, so pointing is also an effective strategy. When in doubt, you can use Google Translate's visual translation tool, or you can just take a picture and ask an LLM to transcribe it for you

Hong Kong

Hong Kong was a British colony until 1997 and English is widely – though certainly not universally – understood. The local variety of Chinese in Hong Kong is Cantonese, a separate, mutually unintelligible language from Mandarin, though Mandarin is taught in schools and generally known by the younger generation. Written Chinese in Hong Kong uses the traditional form of characters rather than the simplified forms used on the mainland. As a traveler, you can comfortably get by with only English.

China presents greater challenges to Western visitors than more familiar destinations, but the challenges are surmountable and the rewards are rich. I look forward to returning to visit again, and I hope that these notes aid you in planning your own travels.
