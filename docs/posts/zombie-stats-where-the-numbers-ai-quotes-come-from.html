Zombie stats: Where the numbers AI quotes come from

1

Following one number back to its source

We asked ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Claude and Google’s AI Overviews 200 questions that call for a number, the kind people ask every day. Then we clicked the citations, and kept clicking. Here is one trail, from the answer to the end.

  1. In the answer

    Every assistant got the same question: “What percentage of salespeople give up after one follow-up?” All five assistants gave the same figure, and only ChatGPT said the evidence behind it was unclear.

  2. One click in

    Google AI Overviews cited a sales firm’s stats page, from 2020. The number is right there. Its “source” link goes to a writing sample.

  3. Two clicks in

    That leads to a freelance writer’s sample article, online by 2017. The number is in its headline. It lists two slide decks, a blog list and two news stories. None is a study of follow-ups.

  4. The end of the trail

    That’s where the trail ends. We tried every other route to this number we could find, and each one stopped at a page that cites no research. This is what we mean by a zombie stat: a number that keeps getting repeated with no study behind it.

Most numbers aren’t like this one. The trouble is that you can’t tell which ones are from the answer. This one came with the same confident tone and the same kind of citation as any other, and all five assistants agreed on it. So we followed 1,285 statistics the same way.

2

Fewer than half lead back to a study

We took a random sample of the statistics each assistant cited, 1,285 in all, and walked every trail the same way: the cited page, then that page’s source, up to five pages deep. Where a page named a source without a link, we searched for it.

40% reached a study that says the same thing. Another 8% reached the study, but the number or its context had changed on the way. The rest never got there: 40% stopped at a source we couldn’t check, because it was locked, gone or silent on the number, and 11% led nowhere at all.

ChatGPT did best, and quoted the fewest numbers: 1.3 per answer, against 6.8 for Claude. The other four landed between 38% and 46%, too close to rank.

To check this wasn’t down to the questions we picked, we also traced 300 statistics from Google AI Overviews answers to real everyday searches. 40% were sourced, about the same share.

3

AI mostly cites pages that repeat the number

The AI mostly wasn’t making numbers up. The page it cited had the figure 82% of the time. But only 21% of the pages cited for a number were the original research.

More than half were company blogs. Add news stories and statistics roundups and you have most of the citations: pages that repeat figures from somewhere else, often without linking to it.

The first page made a big difference. When a trail started at a research firm or a government page, it reached a matching study 61% of the time. From a company blog, 37%; from a statistics roundup, 35%. Even research firms fell short 38% of the time, often because the full report sat behind a form.

A citation tells you where the AI found a number, not where the number came from.

4

Some numbers change along the way

Reaching the study didn’t always mean the number held. Of the statistics that got there, 17% had changed on the way. The digits moved only about one time in four. Usually it was the context: what was measured, the year, the source, or who was asked.

5

The zombie stats

Ask five assistants the same question and you’ll often get five different numbers. On 28 of the 71 questions where all five gave a percentage, we got four or five different figures.

Across those questions, the typical gap between the highest figure and the lowest was 16 points.

When all five agreed, the number wasn’t any more likely to check out. They gave the same figure on 10 questions, and of the 5 we traced, 2 were sourced. The statistics quoted by three or more assistants did no better: 11 of 29 were sourced.

Four were quoted by all five assistants, and we couldn’t find a study behind any of them.

What to do with a statistic from an AI

Treat it as a lead rather than a source. Here’s what we’d do, whether you use these numbers or publish them.

If you use them

  1. 1

    Follow the citation, then follow it again.

    If the cited page is a list of statistics or a company blog, use its own link to go one page further. Keep going until you reach the study itself, or until you can’t.

  2. 2

    Check who was asked, how many, and when.

    Most of the changes we found were in the context, not the digits: one country’s poll, a ten-year-old study, fifteen people.

  3. 3

    Don’t take agreement as a check.

    Five assistants giving the same number isn’t five sources. The numbers they repeated most were no better sourced than the rest.

If you publish them

  1. 1

    Put the method next to the number.

    The sample, the date and a link to the source, on the same page as the figure, so the next page, and the next answer, can trace it back to you.

  2. 2

    Keep the findings in the open.

    Reports behind a form were a common dead end: even trails that started at a research firm fell short 38% of the time. Put the headline numbers and the method on an open page.

  3. 3

    See which page AI cites for your numbers.

    It is often a roundup or a blog that copied you, sometimes with an older figure. That page decides what people hear.
