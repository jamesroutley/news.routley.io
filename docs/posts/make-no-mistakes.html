Make (no) mistakes

It is possible to commit no mistakes and still lose.

That is not a weakness. That is life.

- Captain Jean-Luc Picard (Star Trek, “Peak Performance”)

I’ve got a bone to pick with Patrick Stewart.

In fairness, it’s also with Ian McKellen, Denzel Washington, and most Macbeths of the last hundred years.

They’re saying a line wrong.

Some context before we get into it. At the 20-minute mark of Macbeth, he has already cut a man in half (offstage), received a prophecy of his future kingship (onstage), and informed his wife (by letter). She is 100% on board with speeding up the succession process; he is uncertain.

He leaves the dinner where he is eating with King Duncan, comes on stage alone, and speaks to us:

If it were done when ‘tis done, then ‘twere well

In other words, if killing King Duncan would be the end of it, I wouldn’t be worried about the eternal consequences (i.e. Heaven and Hell). The rest of the speech goes on to worry about being assassinated in return and about how beloved Duncan is, concluding that his ambition is not enough to drive him to commit the murder.

Like most actors, Patrick Stewart takes the first clause as a single thought: “If it were done when ‘tis done, then ‘twere well it were done quickly.” If killing the king were the end of it, I’d better do it quickly.

But this makes no sense with the rest of the speech! At no point is Macbeth worried about timing. Duncan is still at dinner; Macbeth can do nothing until he goes to sleep anyways. Macbeth is worried about consequences.

The core of the speech lives in the first line: if it were done when ‘tis done, then ‘twere well. (Then) it were done quickly1 — in other words, it would be over without further issue. This matches the argument of the speech, wherein Macbeth considers the potential fallout from his considered actions.

Not only is this a better reading, it is also the reading best supported by the First Folio. Published in 1623, about 7 years after Shakespeare’s death, it was the first ever printing of half of Shakespeare’s plays, including The Tempest, Julius Caesar, and of course Macbeth.

Moreover, it was put together by members of Shakespeare’s theater company from the company’s own manuscripts, which makes it the closest thing to a script written for performance. The punctuation is very irregular and often ungrammatical by modern standards—commas can function as semicolons, question marks can be soft exclamation points, and so on—because it’s a text to be played, not a book to be read.2 Modern editions tend to regularize the punctuation, and thereby lose these clues.

But look at it!

I discovered this reading when I was 19, preparing a monologue to audition for the part of Macbeth in a college production.

I had spent the first 18 years of my life caring a lot about being right and getting rewarded for doing so. I figured I would be a physicist, or maybe a mechanical engineer. I had come to like math and science purely because I was good at solving problems, and on some level I thought that this was the highest form of enjoyment available: to be good at stuff.

Then I discovered theater, and found that there was something I was willing — wanting — to do, even though I was unutterably bad at it.

Which meant that, a year later, I was desperately seeking some way in to the theater world that could use my intelligence. Shakespeare’s language is beautiful, but more importantly, it was inaccessible to everyone else.

Just being able to speak Shakespeare accurately is half the battle for most actors, and it came easily for me. Picking apart the sentence structure, puzzling over the punctuation of the Folio: these were things I could do. I could turn Shakespeare into a problem and then solve it.

Armed with my understanding, I performed the monologue “correctly”.

I did not get the part, because I was not “good at acting”.

For years afterwards, I believed I should have gotten that part (and others!), because I knew the text better.

It is possible to commit no mistakes and still lose.

That is because correctness is for nerds.

- Captain Jean-Luc Picard

Patrick Stewart was a great actor who spent his most formative years as a classical actor with the Royal Shakespeare Company. When he moved to TV, fellow actors on Star Trek said his high standards and his aura of principle and integrity influenced the entire cast to prepare more seriously for their work.

In this clip, he makes at least three errors.3

But look at him!

What matters is that, in this moment, we see a human being, balanced on an impossible edge. We see someone torn between desire and loyalty. We see a man carrying vast contradictions, capable of both greatness and cruelty.

We see Macbeth, and we see Patrick Stewart. Exposed, in a moment of doubt and fear, and terribly, unmistakably, alive.

Why was I so attached to correctness? Because you can figure it out before you step on stage. Because you can solve the problem in the comfort of your room. Because if you abandon the armor of correctness, all you can offer is your living self. And for me, that felt, and still feels, almost unbearable.

I never watched Star Trek as a teenager. If I had, I would have been the first to point out that the famous Picard quote, in context, is wrong.

He says it to Data, the ship’s android, who just lost a strategy game. If you lose a game of chess, you made at least one mistake. Maybe not a big blunder, but you didn’t play perfectly.4 And so I would say: It is not actually possible to make no mistakes and still lose, in a game like that.

But if I could reach back to that teenager, I would try to get him to see that the point of the line, the point of writing, and art, is not to get things right.

The point is that sometimes, we need to find the courage to leave the game of correctness behind. That everything worth having is out there, in the red-blood chaos of the living world, where we don’t even get to know which mistakes we’re making, where everything changes every day, where we may lose without ever knowing why.

And that is not a weakness.

That is life.
