Show HN: Uberblok – an interactive museum of ZFS internals

A museum of ZFS, read in its source

Storage has always had the same enemies: disks that lie, writes cut short, a crash in the middle of an update. ZFS answers them with a handful of ideas. This museum shows them one room at a time, from the code that implements them.

Every animation maps to lines of OpenZFS zfs-2.4.4, frozen at commit 71a9f9578616, and every excerpt links to them. Nothing here is drawn from memory.

Why this museum? ↓

The object in the Hall: the uberblock

A pool is millions of blocks spread over several disks. After a crash, the system must find its current, consistent state without scanning any of them. So one small record names the root of everything: the uberblock. It holds a transaction group number, ub_txg, and one block pointer, ub_rootbp; every other block of the pool is reachable from that pointer.

Each disk carries four labels, two at its start and two at its end, and each label keeps a 128 KiB ring of uberblocks. A new uberblock never overwrites the previous one: the uberblock of txg N goes to slot N mod the number of slots (with multihost=on, the last slot is kept for MMP and the ring turns on one slot fewer). On import, ZFS reads every slot of every label and keeps the valid uberblock with the highest txg.

The ring is often said to hold 128 uberblocks. It holds 128 KiB: a slot takes one sector, rounded up to 1 KiB and capped at 8 KiB, so 128 slots on 512-byte sectors, 64 at ashift 11, 32 on the 4K-sector disks of today, 16 beyond. Change the sector size and watch the next txgs take their slots in turn.

Every uberblock starts with the same magic number, 0x00bab10c: “oo-ba-bloc”.

Dedication

Why this museum?

Keeping data safe is critical work, and seldom seen. A file system is handed everything people keep, and it has to stay right through crashes, failing disks and writes cut short. One of the principles ZFS was designed on, in Bill Moore’s words: never, ever trust the underlying hardware.1

This museum pays homage to that piece of engineering, and to the people who built it. Jeff Bonwick led the development of ZFS, with Bill Moore as co-lead;1 Matt Ahrens was one of its two original authors.2 The work began in 2001 with two engineers at Sun Microsystems3 and was integrated into Solaris on 31 October 2005,4 a step Bonwick summed up as 584 files, 92,000 lines of change, 56 patents, 5 years.4 On 17 November he wrote: I’m incredibly proud of the ZFS team, and deeply grateful: no one person can do something on this scale.5

It pays homage, too, to the engineering at Sun in those years, where engineers published how their systems worked: the slab allocator, presented by Jeff Bonwick at USENIX in 1994;6 DTrace, by Bryan Cantrill, Michael Shapiro and Adam Leventhal, in 2004;7 and the source of ZFS itself, released under the CDDL as part of OpenSolaris.3

And to those who carry it on: OpenZFS, announced in 20132 and founded by members of the Linux, FreeBSD, Mac OS X and illumos communities.2 The release this museum reads lists more than 700 people in its AUTHORS file.
