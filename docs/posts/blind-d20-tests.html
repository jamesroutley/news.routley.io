Blind D20 Tests

We join our party at the end of their search for Princess Apple…or so they think!

C: You push past the guard and enter the room. You see a simple bed, nightstand, desk and chair; a woman in plain clothes is making the bed. She’s surprised by your entrance.

B: I brandish my sword, and demand, “Where is the princess!?”

C: Not even going to have you roll Intimidate, she’s terrified. “She’s gone! They took her away yesterday! She’s in another castle!”

A: Of course she is. “Princess Apple,” what else would we expect from Charles?

B: …wait a minute. Is she telling the truth? Is this the princess in disguise?

C: Go ahead and roll Insight.

B: Two on the die, so… uh, one.

A: Ouch.

B: Yeah, not my best skill.

C: This is definitely the princess, trying to put you off her trail.

At this point, B is pretty sure that that this is not the princess… but B’s character is sure it is. The in-character knowledge and out-of-character knowledge differ.

Trust and mystery

In my experience playing TTRPGs, we trust our fellow players to keep the two kinds of knowledge separate, and to make character choices in line with what the character knows only. Still, it can be tempting to “lean” in the direction of the out-of-character knowledge; for one player or another to seek a different way for the party to get the “true” answer. Sometimes those responses can themselves lead to interesting and creative choices!

But sometimes, as a DM, I want to keep some mystery for the player too. Did the enemy’s ritual work, or did you disrupt it in time? You won’t know until later; maybe your ally is now carrying a curse.

In the standard D20 Test formula, it’s hard to create this kind of ambiguity for players. The DM has set the difficulty to something, probably between 10 and 30; if the d20 roll + bonus is high, the player knows they probably succeeded, and vice-versa if it’s low.

Deconstructing the D20 Test

A D20 Test has three parts: a difficulty (AC or DC), a modifier, and a roll from a 20-sided die.

$$ \text{d20} + \text{modifier} \ge \text{difficulty} $$

If the difficulty isn’t a secret, the DM can just tell the player, and the player can report their success or failure. If the DM wants to keep the difficulty secret, they can ask the player for the d20+modifier sum, and describe the results without disclosing the difficulty.

But, as shown above, this is only ambiguous if the player’s sum is close to the DC… and the DC is not usually hard to ballpark. The player can often guess at the result, especially with a high or low roll.

Let’s shuffle the symbols around. If the DM is willing to do a little math in their head, they can ask the player for their modifier and roll separately, and apply the modifier as a decrease of the DC:1

$$ \text{d20} \ge \text{difficulty} - \text{modifier} $$

Everyone still has the same amount of information here; the player can still make a guess based on their roll being high or low. So we’re not done.

We’ll have the DM do a little more work. Before the player rolls, the DM will roll a die; on evens, “high is good”, and on odds, “low is good”:

$$ \begin{align} \\ \text{d20} &\ge \text{difficulty} - \text{modifier} \tag{success on high low} \\ 20 - \text{d20} &\ge \text{difficulty} - \text{modifier} \tag{success on low roll} \\ \end{align} $$

Now the player’s roll doesn’t give them any information about their success; however, the roll is still fair, and has the same distribution as before.

To recap, to do a Blind D20 Test, the DM:

  1. Sets the difficulty
  2. Asks the player for their modifier
  3. Subtracts this from the difficulty
  4. Rolls behind the screen, “high or low”
  5. Asks the player for their d20 roll
    1. On “high”, use this number
    2. On “low”, use 20 - this number
  6. Compares to the difficulty-minus-modifier
  7. Reports what the character perceives, without explicitly communicating success or failure

We’re not made of math

As a DM, I wouldn’t use a Blind D20 Test without explaining it to the players beforehand. After all, “preserving mystery for players” only works if they know not to trust the “high is good” convention.

More importantly, the D20 Test is part of the social contract of the game. If the DM starts deciding success and failure based on novel criteria, without telling the players, they’re technically still playing by “Rule 1”… but in my opinion, that’s the least fun rule in the book. I’d rather explore the narrative together, according to what the DM and players have agreed on.

I wouldn’t use Blind D20 Tests frequently. Lots of the time, the results of a D20 Test are visible to the characters: “you deal damage to the naiad”, “the dragon’s pupils dilate with lust.” Often as a DM I want may players to succeed, even if their characters fail; being open about success or failure helps us build that narrative.

Still, I think Blind D20 Tests are a nice tool to have in your DM toolkit. If you try it out, for good or ill, let me know!

Glossary

Intimidate
In D&D, the Intimidate skill is used to cause another party to feel fear, often in order to get them to do what you want.
Insight
In D&D, the Insight skill is used to ascertain someone’s meaning, including to tell if they are lying.
DM
“Dungeonmaster”. In D&D, the DM is responsible for narration of anything other than the player characters, adjudicating the rules, and generally running the game.
TTRPGs
Table-top role playing games; in this case, Dungeons and Dragons.
D20 Test
The “core mechanic” of Dungeons and Dragons. Roll a 20-sided die, and add a modifier based on your character’s abilities, skills, and resources. Compare the result to a threshold set by the DM; if the result exceeds the threshold, your character succeeds.
AC
Armor Class: the difficulty applied to an attack. A character wearing armor, or a nimble creature, will have a higher AC, i.e. be more difficult to hit and deal damage to.
DC
Difficulty Class: the difficulty applied to an ability check of saving through. For instance, dodging out of the way of a Fireball spell might be a DC 15 Dexterity save.
Rule 1
In D&D, Rule 1 is “the Dungeonmaster is in charge.” This rule is called out at the start of the 5th Edition sourcebooks after years of collective experience of players disputing rules at the table; giving the DM this authority is intended to allow play to progress smoothly.
