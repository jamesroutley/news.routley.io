It's rare that map projections make international news. So last week was pretty exciting for cartographers. The UN endorsed a resolution to promote maps that accurately reflect the true size of continents, dethroning the 500-year-old Mercator projection in the process.

The decision, which was backed by the African Union, was nearly unanimous: 164 countries voted in favour and just a single nation opposed. (Yeah, you guessed it: a classic American Exception™️.)

"Maps shape our understanding of the world," said Togo's foreign minister Robert Dussey before the vote.

For nearly half a millennium, the Mercator map has shaped that understanding around the West — inflating its size and stature at the expense of everywhere else.

Mercator is the most-widely used projection (Google Maps uses it, for one), but it was created in 1569 during the age of exploration. It's mostly good for sea navigation. Any straight line drawn on the map represents a constant compass bearing.

Mercator: A map for sailors

But what's great for navigation isn't so great for accurate geographic representation. Distances and areas are progressively more exaggerated as they move away from the Equator. This is why Greenland looks about the size of Africa. It's actually 14 times smaller.

This disproportionately inflates the size of Europe, Russia and North America. And since we often, without thinking, use size as a proxy for importance or power, this subtly reinforces a sense of northern superiority over the global south.

Criticism of the Mercator projection isn't new. It even came up on one of the greatest TV shows of all time, the West Wing. (Don't email me if you disagree. You're wrong.)

The projection proposed by cartographers in The West Wing is Gall-Peters. It fixes Mercator's core problem by presenting an equal-area perspective, showing the true relative size of countries that are minimized on the Mercator map.

Gall-Peters: A map to 'decolonize the curriculum'

An equal-area perspective means that if a country takes up 1% of the Earth's actual surface area, then it would take up 1% of the map area. But there's a trade-off: it's super wonky. The distortion comes from trying to maintain those equal proportions while representing a spherical globe on a flat piece of paper. Land near the poles gets vertically compressed and land near the equator is vertically stretched.

In other words: Gall-Peters gets the size of continents relative to other continents correct, but the shapes are off.

In 2017, Boston Public Schools acted on The West Wing's suggestion, ditching Mercator for Gall-Peters to 'decolonize the curriculum'. Perhaps unexpectedly, this didn't go over well with the mapping community. Many felt Gall-Peters — with its awkwardly stretched countries — wasn't a great fix either.

Which is how we get to the UN's newest projection-of-preference: Equal Earth. It was created in 2018 directly in response to the Boston Public School cartographic controversy.

Equal Earth: A map for the modern era

Equal Earth maintains the size accuracy of the Gall-Peters projection but without such extreme distortion. While Gall-Peters uses straight lines, Equal Earth echoes the globe's actual shape with curved lines. This distributes the stretching more evenly, though there's still some distortion near the poles.

Comparing the Mercator and Equal Earth projections, we can see how much this affects the size of both Africa and Europe.

Using Equal Earth, Africa appears more true to size

All maps distort the world in some way; it's the nature of trying to project a globe onto a flat plane. This means that maps are a series of choices about what's important. For the UN, the body that represents the countries of the world, those choices go beyond simple geography.

As the resolution itself states: "Correcting cartographic distortions of continental sizes constitutes an act of cognitive justice and memorial reparation."

In the interest of keeping things tight, I cut two map projections from the main piece. But they are too interesting not to share!

Winkel Tripel (1921): It was designed to minimize three kinds of distortion: area, direction and distance. In 1998, National Geographic adopted this projection because it has the lowest distortion scale across one-page flat maps. The trade-offs? It's more computationally complex, and it under- and over-states some country sizes.

Dymaxion (1943): Finally, a map for people who want something unusable! Created by Buckminster Fuller, it unfolds the globe as a polyhedron, showing landmasses as one connected system. Actually, anthropologists like this one because it helps to visualize continental relationships and historical migrations.
