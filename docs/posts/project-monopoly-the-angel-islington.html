MONOPOLY BOARD PROPERTY NUMBER 3: THE ANGEL ISLINGTON

(Details of books and maps at the end of the post)

The Angel Islington, unlike the other properties, is not named after a street.

Why Victor Watson [managing director of board game maker Waddingtons] incongruously named one place on the Monopoly board after a tavern and not a street is a little unclear. The Waddington company history recounts that he had tea there with his secretary Marjory Phillips at the end of a taxi ride around London and they chose it rather than looking further. From ‘No Free Parking’.

A section from my map showing King’s Cross Station in the centre, with Euston Road and Pentonville Road running west to east in a straight line past the front of the station. As you head east towards the end of Pentonville Road you reach Angel tube station. All three properties are coloured light blue on the Monopoly board, starting with The Angel Islington, followed non-logically by Euston Road then Pentonville Road.

HOW TO GET HERE

For transport options, Citymapper is invaluable as a tool to get from A to B. I took the Northern Line to Angel tube station.

From Upper Street there are buses that go along Pentonville Road and then on to Euston Road. I will write about this in a future post for anyone who wishes to cover the three light blue Monopoly properties on the same day.

 

ASPECTS OF MY VISIT

The last time I visited this area was at the end of May 2025, to buy some new dining room chairs from ARIA. We started the day with coffee and a matcha latte at Katsute 100. After ordering the chairs we walked around, had some unmemorable noodles for lunch (so sad!) and visited Borough Kitchen. I thought the area was worth another visit, but here we are a year later and if not for this Monopoly  project, I would not have ventured to Angel again. Not for lack of enthusiasm but for lack of time, and also there is so much else to see in the rest of London.

The Angel itself was transformed from a sedate Regency tavern to a Victorian public house with ‘glittering plate glass’ and billiard rooms and then to an Edwardian hotel with biscuit- coloured terracotta facing, a green marble staircase and a lavishly baroque brown dome. In 1921, it evolved again from a hotel to a Lyons Café, though within the same Edwardian building. If Marjory Phillips did take tea in the Angel, before persuading Victor Watson of its merits as a stop on the Monopoly board, it was in the Lyons incarnation that she took her refreshments. From ‘No Free Parking’.

The site of the Lyons Café, 1 Islington High Street, was until recently a bank. It is now a German bakery, Zeit für Brot (meaning Time for Bread), and so fitting for refreshment stop. Better still, on the opposite side is a beautiful space within an office block that houses Frequency Coffee.

For other recommended coffee shops, European Coffee Trip is a useful. We are spoilt for choice.
